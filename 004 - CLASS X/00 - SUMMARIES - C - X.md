## [A Letter To God](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2001%20-%20A%20LETTER%20TO%20GOD.md)

### Flowchart

Drought threatens Lencho’s crop → rain brings hope → hail destroys the harvest → Lencho asks God for 100 pesos → postmaster and staff collect 70 pesos → Lencho receives it and accuses the post-office workers of stealing the rest

### Summary

**LENCHO'S HOPE OF GOOD HARVEST**

Lencho, an “ox of a man” who works like an animal, lives with his family in the valley’s only house, above ripe corn and flowers promising harvest; he watches the north-east for rain. The air turns “fresh and sweet.”

**LENCHO'S HOPE TURNED INTO DESPAIR**

Lencho enjoys the drops, calling them “new coins” (large ones ten-cent pieces, small ones fives); hailstones look like “new silver coins” and “frozen pearls.” In an hour hail destroys the corn and flowers, strips trees, and leaves the valley “white, as if covered with salt”; Lencho says locusts would have left more.

**LENCHO'S FIRM BELIEF IN GOD**

Though his family fears hunger, Lencho’s sole hope is God, whose eyes see even into consciences. He writes asking for 100 pesos to resow and live until harvest.

**THE POSTMASTER'S REACTION TO LENCHO'S LETTER**

The postman laughs at the address; the amiable postmaster admires Lencho’s faith, then asks employees and friends for donations and adds part of his salary. Unable to raise 100 pesos, he sends 70 with the single-word signature “God.”

**LENCHO RECIEVES THE MONEY**

Next Sunday Lencho shows no surprise at the money—such is his confidence—but becomes angry when he counts only 70 pesos; he is certain God cannot make a mistake or deny his request.

**LENCHO WRITES ANOTHER LETTER**

Lencho writes again, requesting the remaining 30 pesos and asking God not to send it by post: the employees, he concludes, are “a bunch of crooks.” The postmaster opens and reads this letter.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Simile / imagery:** Lencho calls the raindrops “new coins” (large drops = ten-cent pieces; small ones = fives); hailstones resemble “new silver coins” and “frozen pearls.” The hail-covered field is “white, as if covered with salt.”
- **Irony:** The postmaster and employees collect money to protect Lencho’s faith, but Lencho calls these helpers “a bunch of crooks,” believing God sent the money and the post office stole the balance. **Character metaphor:** Lencho is “an ox of a man,” stressing his strength and hard work.

## [Nelson Mandela: Long Walk To Freedom](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2004%20-%20NELSON%20MANDELA.md)

### Flowchart

Mandela’s inauguration marks the end of apartheid → he honours those who struggled for freedom → reflects on courage, oppression and the oppressor’s loss of humanity → explains that people are born with love, not hatred → commits to a life of service and shared liberty

### Summary

**INSTALLATION OF THE FIRST DEMOCRATIC GOVERNMENT OF SOUTH AFRICA**

On 10 May 1994, in Pretoria’s sandstone Union Buildings amphitheatre—the former seat of white supremacy—Zenani accompanies Mandela as he is sworn in after De Klerk (second deputy) and Mbeki (first deputy). Mandela pledges to uphold the Constitution and devote himself to the Republic and its people; a “rainbow gathering” witnesses the first democratic, non-racial government.

**MANDELA ADDRESSES THE GUESTS**

Mandela thanks international guests for a shared victory for justice, peace and human dignity; he pledges to free people from poverty, deprivation, suffering and discrimination, and to build a society of which humanity can be proud.

**DISPLAY OF THE MILITARY POWER BY SOUTH AFRICAN JETS**

Jets, helicopters and troop carriers fly in precise formation; generals who once might have arrested Mandela now salute him. Impala jets leave the new flag’s colours in smoke. Whites and blacks sing the two anthems, symbolising reconciliation; both groups will learn the once-despised anthem.

**APARTHEID AND SOUTH AFRICA**

Mandela traces apartheid to white rule’s racial domination, a harsh, inhumane system now overturned; its “deep and lasting wound” will take generations to heal. The new order recognises every person’s rights regardless of skin colour.

**MANDELA RECALLS THE SACRIFICES OF FREEDOM FIGHTERS**

Mandela sees himself as “the sum” of thousands of sacrificed patriots—Oliver Tambo, Walter Sisulu, Chief Luthuli, Yusuf Dadoo, Bram Fischer and Robert Sobukwe—and regrets they cannot witness freedom. Oppression produced exceptional courage and generosity; the people, “finer and truer than the purest diamonds,” are South Africa’s greatest wealth. Courage is triumph over fear.

**MAN'S NATURAL GOODNESS**

No one is born hating others because of race or religion; people can be taught to love.

**TWIN OBLIGATIONS OF MAN**

Every person has twin duties to family and country; apartheid punished Black people and forced those serving their people from home into a “twilight existence” of secrecy and rebellion. Mandela did not choose family over country, but could not fulfil both roles.

**MANDELA'S CONCEPT OF FREEDOM**

As a boy, freedom meant running, swimming, roasting mealies and riding bulls within tribal custom; as a student, it meant staying out, reading and going where he chose; later it meant fulfilling his potential, earning, marrying and raising a family. He realises childhood freedom was an illusion.

**MANDELA JOINS AFRICAN NATIONAL CONGRESS**

Seeing his people’s freedom curtailed, Mandela joins the ANC; his personal “hunger” becomes a hunger for collective dignity and self-respect. The struggle turns a frightened man bold, attorney into criminal, family-loving husband homeless and life-loving man “like a monk”; freedom is indivisible—others’ chains bind him too.

**MANDELA'S REALISATION**

The oppressor is also a “prisoner of hatred,” locked behind prejudice; Mandela glimpses a guard’s humanity even in prison. Goodness is “a flame that can be hidden but never extinguished.” Both oppressor and oppressed are robbed of humanity and must be liberated.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Metaphor / symbolism:** The inauguration creates a “rainbow gathering” (racial and national unity); apartheid leaves a “deep and lasting wound”; the freedom struggle’s constraints are “chains.”
- **Metaphor:** Mandela calls people “finer and truer than the purest diamonds”—the nation’s greatest wealth. “Courage” is the “triumph” over fear; goodness is “a flame that can be hidden but never extinguished.”
- **Imagery:** A person separated from family under apartheid lives a “twilight existence of secrecy and rebellion.” Mandela says the oppressor is a “prisoner of hatred,” “locked behind the bars” of prejudice; both oppressed and oppressor lose humanity.

## [Two Stories About Flying](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2006%20-%20TWO%20STORIES%20ABOUT%20FLYING.md)

### I. His First Flight — Flowchart

Young seagull fears flying while his siblings take off → family encourages him, then leaves him hungry → mother tempts him with food → he dives, spreads his wings and flies → family celebrates his first flight

### II. The Black Aeroplane — Flowchart

Pilot flies toward England through a storm → instruments fail and fuel runs low → mysterious black aeroplane guides him to safety → he lands → control tower reports no other aircraft was in the sky

### Summary — I. His First Flight

**THE YOUNG SEAGULL'S FEAR OF FLYING**

His two brothers and sister flew the previous day; despite having longer wings, the young seagull fears the “miles” of sea below and retreats to his hole. Parents call, scold and threaten starvation, but he cannot move.

**PARENTS LEAVE THE YOUNG SEAGULL ALONE**

After 24 hours alone, he watches his parents teach his siblings to skim waves and dive; his brother catches a herring and is praised, while the family taunts his cowardice.

**THE HUNGRY SEAGULL**

On the sun-baked southern ledge, hungry since nightfall, he pretends to sleep; his siblings doze, his father preens, and only his mother watches while tearing fish and sharpening her beak on the rock.

**THE MOTHER'S TRICK**

His mother flies toward him with fish, then halts within beak’s reach; maddened by hunger, he dives, falls in terror, then feels wind under his wings and begins soaring.

**THE JOY OF FLIGHT**

His heart “stands still” in terror, but he feels air beneath his wings, soars and flaps upward; he forgets he ever feared flying and practises diving, curving and soaring.

**THE FAMILY'S CELEBRATION**

The family circles, “curveting and banking”; over the green sea he tries to land, sinks to his belly, then floats. His family praises him and offers dogfish scraps: he has made his first flight.

### Summary — II. The Black Aeroplane

**THE NARRATOR'S TRIP TO ENGLAND**

At 1:30 a.m. the narrator’s old Dakota flies from France to England under moon and stars; Paris Control directs him 12° west. With the second and last fuel tank, he expects to reach breakfast with his family.

**APPEARANCE OF STORM CLOUDS**

150 km beyond Paris, huge storm clouds look like “black mountains.” Unable to fly over or around them and reluctant to return to Paris, he risks the storm to get home.

**A RISKY BUT BRAVE DECISION**

In total darkness the Dakota jumps and twists; compass spins, other instruments and radio fail, leaving him lost.

**A BLACK AEROPLANE APPEARS**

A black plane without wing lights appears beside him; its pilot waves and signals him to follow, turning north ahead of the Dakota.

**NARRATOR FOLLOWS BLACK AEROPLANE**

After half an hour, with 5–10 minutes’ fuel left, the black plane descends; the narrator follows out of the clouds to runway lights and lands safely. His “friend” has vanished.

**THE BLACK AEROPLANE REMAINS A MYSTERY**

The control-centre woman says no other aircraft flew that night; the rescuer remains a mystery.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Simile / imagery (His First Flight):** The young bird’s fear contrasts with the family’s flight; his mother’s fish lure converts fear into action. The family’s celebration marks the change from “cowardice” to confidence.
- **Simile (The Black Aeroplane):** Storm clouds look “like black mountains”; the narrator follows the unknown pilot “like an obedient child.” The black plane’s unexplained disappearance leaves the rescue mysterious.

## [From the Diary of Anne Frank](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2009%20-%20FROM%20THE%20DIARY%20OF%20ANNE%20FRANK.md)

### Flowchart

Anne feels she lacks a true confidante → begins writing to her diary, Kitty → reflects on her family and school life → Mr Keesing punishes her for talking → Anne answers with humorous essays and a poem → Mr Keesing accepts her talkative nature

### Summary

**ANNE'S NEED TO WRITE A DIARY**

Thirteen-year-old Anne receives a diary from her father; lacking a confidante despite family and friends, she begins writing in June 1942 and names it Kitty.

**ANNE'S CHILDHOOD AND FAMILY**

Anne adores her father Otto; he married Edith at 36 when she was 25. Margot was born in 1926, Anne in 1929; after the parents moved to Holland in 1933, the girls joined them from Aachen.

**ANNE'S SCHOOLING**

Anne attends Montessori school until six; at year’s end she and headmistress Mrs Kuperus weep during a “heartbreaking farewell.”

**ANNE'S GRANDMOTHER'S DEATH**

Anne loves her grandmother, who becomes ill and has an operation in 1941, then dies in January 1942; Anne lights a candle for her on her birthday.

**ANNE'S SCHOOL RESULT AND HER VIEWS ABOUT TEACHERS**

On 20 June 1942 the class “quakes in its boots” over promotion; Anne expects to pass but worries about Maths, laughs at C.N. and Jacques betting their holiday savings, and calls teachers “the most unpredictable creatures on earth.”

**ANNE'S TALKATIVE NATURE AND PUNISHMENT BY MR KEESING**

Anne gets along pretty well with her teachers but talks in Mr Keesing’s Maths class; he assigns “A Chatterbox” as punishment.

**COMPLETION OF EXTRA HOMEWORK**

Anne writes three pages: talking is a student’s trait; she will control it, but cannot cure it because her equally talkative mother passed it on.

**MORE PUNISHMENTS FOR ANNE**

Keesing laughs at “A Chatterbox”; when Anne talks again he assigns “An Incorrigible Chatterbox,” then “Quack, Quack, Quack, Said Mistress Chatterbox.” Anne thinks he is joking at her expense.

**ANNE MOCKS MR KEESING AMUSINGLY WITH HER HOMEWORK**

With poetically gifted Sanne, Anne writes in verse of a father swan biting three talkative ducklings to death. Keesing reads it to several classes, takes the joke well, stops assigning extra work and starts joking himself.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Personification / metaphor:** “Paper has more patience than people”—Anne treats her diary as a trusted friend, Kitty, and confidante.
- **Idioms / imagery:** “Quaking in its boots” describes the class’s exam-result anxiety; “teachers are the most unpredictable creatures on earth” is Anne’s humorous exaggeration.
- **Humour / irony:** Anne says she was “plunked down on the table as a birthday present” for Margot. Her duck poem turns Mr Keesing’s punishment into a joke; he enjoys it and stops punishing her for talking.

## [Glimpses of India](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2011%20-%20GLIMPSES%20OF%20INDIA.md)

### I. A Baker from Goa — Flowchart

Portuguese baking tradition remains in Goa → village baker visits daily → children eagerly await bread-bangles → bread is essential for ceremonies and festivals → baker’s dress and earnings reflect his respected place in village life

### II. Coorg — Flowchart

Coorg’s hills, forests, coffee and spices are introduced → account describes Kodavu ancestry, customs and bravery → region’s martial tradition is highlighted → rivers, wildlife and outdoor adventures make Coorg a place to visit

### III. Tea from Assam — Flowchart

Pranjol and Rajvir travel to Assam → Rajvir admires the tea gardens and shares tea legends → they reach Pranjol’s family tea estate → Rajvir observes tea-pluckers and explains the second-flush season

### Summary — I. A Baker from Goa

**THE OLD PORTUGUESE DAYS IN GOA**

Portuguese baking traditions survive in Goa: *paders*, ovens, mixers and moulders still make bread, and the baker’s bamboo staff remains familiar.

**THE TRADITIONAL BAKER DURING THE NARRATOR'S CHILDHOOD**

In the narrator’s childhood, the baker visits at least twice daily; his bamboo’s thud and jingle wake the children, who run for bread-bangles and sweet bread—not the loaves bought by the maids.

**THE BAKER'S ARRIVAL**

With “jhang, jhang,” he balances the basket, taps his staff, greets the housewife and gives loaves to the maid; children climb benches or parapets to peer in, recalling the loaves’ fragrance and eating before brushing.

**IMPORTANCE OF BREAD IN TRADITIONAL CEREMONIES**

Sweet *bol* is a marriage gift; bread completes feasts, sandwiches are made for engagements, and cakes and *bolinhas* are essential at Christmas and festivals.

**THE BAKER'S DRESS AND MONTHLY ACCOUNTS**

The old *kabai* is a knee-length, one-piece frock; later bakers wear shorter-than-full, longer-than-half trousers. Bills are pencilled on a wall monthly; a plump, “jackfruit-like” build testifies to prosperity.

**BAKING : A PROFITABLE PROFESSION**

Baking is profitable: baker, family and servants are well-fed and happy; traditional furnaces remain and sons continue the family trade after fathers die.

### Summary — II. COORG

**A HEAVEN CALLED COORG**

Coorg (Kodagu), Karnataka’s smallest district, lies midway between Mysore and Mangalore; rolling hills are home to a proud, martial people, beautiful women and wild creatures—a “piece of heaven.”

**WEATHER AND ENVIRONMENT OF COORG**

Evergreen forests cover 30% of Coorg; monsoon rain keeps many visitors away. September–March is pleasant, with coffee-scented air and colonial bungalows under tree canopies.

**THE ORIGIN OF PEOPLE OF COORG**

One legend traces Kodavus to Alexander’s army, which settled after return became impractical; another suggests Arab descent. Distinct martial, marriage and religious customs include the *kuppia*, like Arab/Kurd *kuffia*.

**HOSPITALITY AND BRAVERY TALES OF KODAVUS**

Kodavus are hospitable and renowned for bravery; the Coorg Regiment is highly decorated, General Cariappa was Coorgi, and Kodavus may carry firearms without a licence.

**RIVER KAVERI AND WILDLIFE IN COORG**

The Kaveri rises in Coorg’s hills and forests; Mahaseer fish thrive there, kingfishers dive, squirrels and langurs drop fruit into the water, and mahouts bathe elephants in the river.

**TOURISM IN COORG**

Visitors can raft, canoe, rappel, rock-climb, mountain-bike and trek. Wildlife includes birds, bees, butterflies, macaques, Malabar squirrels, langurs and slender loris; the narrator gives wild elephants space.

**REACHING COORG**

Madikeri is the gateway; Coorg is about 250–260 km from Bengaluru by road (most travel via Mysore). Nearest airports: Mangalore (135 km) and Bengaluru (260 km); railheads: Mysore, Mangalore and Hassan. Brahmagiri offers a panoramic misty view; a rope bridge reaches 64-acre Nisargadhama; nearby Bylakuppe is India’s largest Tibetan settlement.

### Summary — III. TEA FROM ASSAM

**PRANJOL AND RAJVIR VISIT TO ASSAM**

Pranjol, an Assamese classmate whose father manages an Upper Assam tea garden, invites Rajvir to visit during summer vacation; they travel by train.

**POPULARITY OF TEA AND TEA GARDENS**

A vendor cries “Chai-garam”; Rajvir notes that over 80 crore cups of tea are drunk worldwide each day. Green paddy gives way to a “sea” of tea bushes, shade trees and “doll-like” pluckers; smoke rises from a factory. Assam has the world’s largest concentration of plantations; Pranjol, born there, reads his detective book.

**RAJVIR'S KNOWLEDGE OF TEA**

Rajvir tells two legends: leaves from burning twigs fall into a Chinese emperor’s boiling water; Bodhidharma cuts off his sleepy eyelids and tea plants grow. Tea was drunk in China by 2700 BC; “tea,” “chai” and “chini” derive from Chinese; it reached Europe in the 16th century as medicine.

**AT THE DHEKIABARI TEA PLANTATION**

At Mariani, Pranjol’s parents drive them to Dhekiabari Tea Estate; they cross a cattle bridge. Pruned bushes line the gravel road; apron-wearing pluckers harvest new leaves. Rajvir identifies the May–July second flush as the best tea, impressing Mr Barua.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Imagery / sound:** The baker makes a “musical entry” with the bamboo’s “jhang, jhang”; the narrator remembers the loaves’ “typical fragrance.” “The tiger never brushed his teeth” is playful, humorous exaggeration.
- **Simile:** A prosperous baker’s “jackfruit-like” appearance humorously signals the family’s well-fed prosperity.
- **Metaphor / personification (Coorg):** Coorg is “a piece of heaven” that “must have drifted from the kingdom of God”; its coffee-scented air “breathes.”
- **Visual imagery (Tea from Assam):** A “sea of tea bushes” stretches to the horizon; shade trees dwarf the plants and tea-pluckers appear as “doll-like figures.”

## [Mijbil the Otter](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2013%20-%20MIJBIL%20THE%20OTTER.md)

### Flowchart

Maxwell decides to keep an otter → receives Mijbil in Iraq → Mijbil adapts, plays and learns routines → they travel to England by air → Mij escapes his box and causes a commotion → they reach London, where Mij attracts public curiosity

### Summary

**CHOOSING AN OTTER AS PET**

After his dog dies, Maxwell decides on an otter; a friend recommends one from the Tigris marshes.

**MAXWELL AND HIS FRIEND TRAVEL TO BASRA**

Maxwell and a friend go to Basra’s Consulate-General for European mail; Maxwell’s mail is delayed, so his friend leaves before the otter arrives.

**MAXWELL'S MAIL ARRIVES**

After a cable and repeated failed phone attempts, Maxwell’s mail arrives five days later; in his bedroom two Arabs present the squirming sack and his friend’s note: “Here is your otter.”

**THE OTTER ARRIVES**

The muddy, pointed-scaled animal resembles a small medieval dragon; a month later Maxwell sees its soft brown fur. He names it Mijbil; zoologists identify the previously unknown race as *Lutrogale perspicillata maxwelli* (“Maxwell’s otter”).

**MIJBIL'S ACTIVITIES**

After 24 hours aloof, Mij sleeps in the crook of Maxwell’s knees, then delights in water, overturning or splashing any bowl. He learns to turn the bath tap; plays “soccer” with a rubber ball and juggles marbles on his belly.

**MIJBIL BECOMES FRIENDLY**

Initially aloof, Mij gradually trusts Maxwell, becomes affectionate and follows him closely.

**TAKING MIJBIL BACK TO HOME**

Dreading transport, Maxwell finds the British airline will not carry animals; he books via Paris and must use an 18-inch-square box on the floor at his feet.

**MIJBIL AND THE BOX**

Left to get used to the box, Mij shreds its lining; blood trickles through the airholes. Maxwell removes sharp edges, then—with ten minutes before takeoff and the airport five miles away—puts him back in.

**MIJBIL IN THE FLIGHT**

A driver races to the airport “like a ricochetting bullet”; angry officials rush Maxwell aboard. The “very queen” of an air hostess lets Mij sit on Maxwell’s knee.

**MIJBIL GOES OUT OF THE BOX**

Mij darts down the plane; a woman screams “A rat!” Maxwell misses him under a turbaned passenger and gets curry on his face. Mij returns, chittering, and nuzzles Maxwell’s face and neck.

**MIJBIL IN LONDON**

In London, Maxwell takes Mij home by taxi; the driver assumes the otter is a baby seal.

**MIJBIL PLAYS OUTSIDE THE HOUSE**

In London, Mij invents a ping-pong game on a sloping suitcase, ambushes the ball, and gallops along the school wall on the way home. On walks he follows compulsive paving-stone and railing rituals.

**PEOPLE COULD NOT RECOGNISE MIJBIL**

Londoners guess baby seal, squirrel, walrus, hippo, beaver, bear cub, leopard and brontosaur; one labourer demands, “what is that supposed to be?”

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Simile / imagery:** Mij first looks like a tiny “medievally-conceived dragon,” covered in “mud armour”; beneath it is “velvet fur like that of a chocolate-brown mole.”
- **Humorous comparisons:** Otters are “as common as mosquitoes”; Mij moves a ball “like a four-footed soccer player.” His water play makes “enough slosh and splash for a hippo.”
- **Simile / hyperbole:** The car driver speeds through Basra “like a ricochetting bullet”; Maxwell calls the helpful air hostess “the very queen of her kind.”
- **Personification / humour:** Londoners’ guesses arrive as a “continuous barrage” of questions; Maxwell “awards” the labourer’s mistaken guess the “highest score.”

## [Madam Rides the Bus](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2015%20-%20MADAM%20RIDES%20THE%20BUS.md)

### Flowchart

Valli becomes fascinated by the bus → gathers details and saves the fare → secretly rides to town alone → refuses help and explores from her seat → declines to get off at the town → sees the same cow dead on the return journey → comes home thoughtful, keeping the trip to herself

### Summary

**VALLIAMMAI AND HER PASTIME**

Eight-year-old Valliammai (“Valli”), with no same-age playmates, loves watching the bus pass hourly; its changing passengers bring “unending joy.” Jealous of friends’ trips, she calls them “Proud!” though they do not know its meaning.

**VALLI WANTS TO TRAVEL IN THE BUS**

Valli longs to ride to the nearby town alone and carefully plans the trip.

**VALLI DISCOVERS DETAILS ABOUT THE BUS JOURNEY**

By listening and asking discreet questions, Valli learns the town is six miles away, fare 30 paise each way, ride 45 minutes; the one-o’clock bus can return by about 2:45.

**VALLI TRAVELLED IN THE BUS**

She saves 60 paise, resisting peppermints, toys, balloons and even a ride on the village-fair merry-go-round; she slips out during her mother’s 1–4 p.m. nap.

**VALLI THOUGHT ABOUT HER PLANNING FOR THE JOURNEY**

Valli has calculated and replanned carefully; on a spring afternoon she calls the departing bus back, pays and boards alone, refusing the conductor’s helping hand.

**VIEW OUTSIDE THE BUS**

The new bus has silver-shining bars and soft seats; Valli peers over the blind at canal, palms, mountains and “green, green, green” fields. A frightened cow runs ahead as the bus honks; she laughs until tears come.

**PASSENGERS INSIDE THE BUS**

The jolly conductor calls her “madam”; Valli rejects the label but enjoys the joke. She insists she is eight, not a child, and stands despite warnings about sharp turns and bumps.

**AN ELDERLY WOMAN IN THE BUS**

A betel-chewing woman with large ear-holes, ugly earrings and juice at her lips asks if Valli is alone and probes for her destination; Valli finds her repulsive and tells her to mind her business.

**THE BUS JOURNEY**

Valli watches the road; the bus seems to gobble vehicles while trees appear to run toward it, stop, then rush away. At town, she stays aboard—too afraid to explore alone—and refuses the conductor’s free cold drink.

**THE BUS ENTERED THE TOWN**

She pays another 30 paise, saying she only wanted a bus ride; the conductor jokes and waits for the return departure. (She does not ask him to keep the trip secret.)

**VALLI SAW A DEAD COW**

The same cow that had made her laugh now lies dead by the road, struck by a vehicle; the sight haunts Valli and she no longer looks outside.

**VALLI RETURNS HOME**

Valli reaches home unnoticed as her mother chats with her aunt; hearing them discuss things unknown outside their world, Valli agrees many things happen without their knowledge. Her aunt calls her a grown-up lady; Valli smiles, knowing they do not know her adventure.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Metaphor / personification:** Valli’s “tiny wish” “crept into her head and grew”; bus passengers’ faces “kindle” her longings, dreams and hopes.
- **Imagery / repetition:** The view is described as “green, green, green,” conveying Valli’s delight; the bus’s shining bars are “like silver.”
- **Contrast / symbol:** The lively cow first makes Valli laugh, then appears dead on the return. This sudden contrast introduces her to death and changes the mood from excitement to sadness.

## [The Sermon at Benares](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2017%20-%20THE%20SERMON%20AT%20BENARES.md)

### Flowchart

Siddhartha witnesses suffering and leaves royal life → seeks enlightenment and becomes the Buddha → Kisa Gotami grieves for her dead son → Buddha asks her to find mustard seeds from a home untouched by death → she finds no such home → understands death is universal and learns to face grief

### Summary

**GAUTAMA'S EARLY LIFE**

Born prince in northern India, Siddhartha studies sacred scriptures at 12, marries at 16 and lives royally for ten years with a son.

**GAUTAMA FEELS SUFFERINGS OF THE WORLD**

At about 25, previously shielded from suffering, he sees a sick man, an aged man, a funeral procession and a monk begging alms; these sights move him to seek enlightenment.

**GAUTAMA SEEKS ENLIGHTENMENT**

After seven years of wandering, he sits beneath a peepal tree and attains enlightenment after seven days; naming it the Bodhi (Wisdom) Tree, he becomes the Buddha and preaches first at Benares.

**STORY OF KISA GOTAMI**

Kisa carries her only dead son door to door asking for medicine; a man sends her to the Buddha. He asks for mustard seed from a house untouched by death of a child, spouse, parent or friend; every home has lost someone.

**CERTAINTY OF DEATH**

Weary, Kisa watches city lights flicker out and understands lives do likewise; she calls her grief selfish and sees death is universal. Buddha compares mortals to falling ripe fruit and breakable clay pots; relatives cannot save the dead, who are led away “like an ox” to slaughter. Weeping deepens suffering; drawing out grief’s “arrow” brings composure and peace.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Metaphor / imagery:** City lights that “flickered up and were extinguished” mirror human lives; Kisa sees life’s briefness and death’s universality.
- **Similes:** Mortals are like “ripe fruits” ready to fall and “earthen vessels” destined to break—images of life’s fragility and inevitable death.
- **Metaphor / simile:** Grief is an “arrow” to be drawn out; the dead are carried off “like an ox that is led to the slaughter.” These images stress that lamentation cannot prevent death or bring peace.

## [The Proposal](LITERATURE/01%20-%20Main/C10%20-%20M%20-%2019%20-%20THE%20PROPOSAL.md)

### Flowchart

Lomov visits to propose to Natalya → they quarrel over the Oxen Meadows → Chubukov joins the argument → Natalya learns Lomov came to propose and urges him back → they quarrel over their dogs → Lomov collapses → Chubukov gets them engaged, and they resume arguing

### Summary

**LOMOV AT CHUBUKOV'S HOUSE**

Nervous 35-year-old Lomov visits Chubukov formally to ask for Natalya’s hand; Chubukov first suspects a loan request.

**CHUBUKOV'S REACTION TO THE PROPOSAL**

When Lomov reveals the proposal, Chubukov is delighted and welcomes the match.

**LOMOV THINKS ABOUT NATALYA AND HIS LIFE**

Alone, Lomov lists Natalya’s qualities but worries about his age, palpitations and nervousness; marriage seems necessary.

**NATALYA MEETS LOMOV**

Natalya enters in an apron while shelling peas, unaware of Lomov’s proposal; he nervously circles through family ties and mentions their adjoining lands, the Oxen Meadows.

## [The Making of a Scientist](LITERATURE/02%20-%20Supplementary/C10%20-%20S%20-%2006%20-%20THE%20MAKING%20OF%20A%20SCIENTIST.md)

### Flowchart

Ebright’s curiosity and his mother’s support nurture learning → butterfly collecting leads to monarch research → experiments and setbacks teach him to ask deeper questions → hard work and collaboration produce important scientific findings → curiosity, effort and a clear purpose make him a scientist

### Summary

**RICHARD'S SUCCESS AT YOUNG AGE**

At 22, Ebright and roommate James R. Wong publish a new cell-work theory in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Science*—the first college students’ work published there; it begins a long string of achievements.

**RICHARD'S CHILDHOOD**

An only child in rural Pennsylvania, unable to play team sports, Ebright collects butterflies from kindergarten, plus rocks, fossils and coins; he also stargazes all night. By grade two he has collected all 25 local butterfly species.

**RICHARD'S MOTHER : HIS TRUE COMPANION**

After his father dies in Richard’s third-grade year, his mother becomes his companion, brings home friends, plans nightly learning, takes trips and supplies telescopes, microscopes, cameras and materials.

**RICHARD READS 'THE TRAVELS OF MONARCH X' BOOK**

At risk of losing interest after collecting all local species, Ebright receives *The Travels of Monarch X*, about migration to Central America; it opens science to him and leads to Dr Urquhart’s tagging invitation.

**TAGGING BUTTERFLIES FOR RESEARCH**

Ebright raises monarchs from eggs in his basement, thousands across life stages, tags and releases them; years of work yield only two recaptures within 75 miles, so he finds tagging tedious.

**RICHARD'S ENTRY IN THE COUNTY SCIENCE FAIR**

In seventh grade he loses with microscope slides of frog tissues; seeing winners conduct real experiments, he decides to test a question, asks Urquhart for ideas and receives suggestions.

**RICHARD'S EIGHTH GRADE PROJECT**

In eighth grade, he tests whether beetles carry a virus that kills monarch caterpillars; results are inconclusive, but he wins for showing he tried a real experiment.

**RICHARD'S NEXT PROJECT ON VICEROY BUTTERFLIES**

In ninth grade, he tests whether viceroys mimic bad-tasting monarchs: a starling rejects ordinary food but eats monarchs. Ebright concludes birds do not avoid monarchs; the project places first in zoology, third overall (later research supports mimicry).

**RICHARD'S DISCOVERY OF A HORMONE**

In high school, he investigates the monarch pupa’s twelve gold spots, assumed ornamental; with another student he builds a device proving they produce a hormone needed for full development. He wins county first, international zoology third and a summer lab opportunity.

**RICHARD CONTINUES HIS RESEARCH ON MONARCH PUPA**

As a junior he continues pupa experiments and wins first at the International Science Fair; as a senior he grows wing cells, proving they develop into normal scales only when fed the gold-spot hormone; the project wins international zoology first.

**RICHARD'S STUDY OF CELL AND DNA**

After identifying the hormone’s chemical structure in a Department of Agriculture lab, Ebright sees its X-ray images and links them to how cells “read” DNA’s blueprint. He and Harvard roommate James R. Wong build plastic molecule models overnight and co-write the theory.

**HIGHEST HONOURS IN HARVARD**

He graduates from Harvard with highest honours, second among 1,510, and becomes a Harvard Medical School graduate researcher testing the theory, which may help explain life and prevent some cancers and diseases.

**RICHARD: AN ALL ROUNDER**

Beyond science, Ebright is a champion debater and public speaker, canoeist, outdoorsman and expert nature/science photographer; straight-A learning leaves energy for debate and Model UN.

**RICHARD: A TRUE SCIENTIST**

Teacher Richard A. Weiherer admires Ebright’s extra effort and competition “for the right reasons”—doing the best job, not winning for prizes. The “recipe” for a scientist: first-rate mind, curiosity and will to win rightly.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Simile / sports metaphor:** Ebright’s publication is compared to “making the big leagues at the age of fifteen” and hitting a “home run” at his first at-bat—a metaphor for exceptional early achievement, not his actual age (22).
- **Metaphor:** *The Travels of Monarch X* “opened the world of science” to Ebright; his growth as a scientist starts with curiosity and collecting butterflies.
- **Extended metaphor:** The conclusion describes a scientist as a recipe: “Start with a first-rate mind, add curiosity, and mix in the will to win for the right reasons.”

After vitamins, the crew reads *Mother Goose*: “Mistress Mary,” then “Hey Diddle Diddle.” Oop laughs at dishes running away; Think-Tank reads rhymes literally.

**MIGHTY THINK-TANK IS ALARMED**

Think-Tank imagines Earth grows silver and explosives, sends cows to the moon and trains animals in music and space travel; Humpty Dumpty’s “great fall” and egg-shaped face seem to predict his capture.

**THINK-TANK CALLS OFF THE INVASION**

Panicked Think-Tank calls it an invasion of Mars, orders a capsule and the whole planet evacuated to Alpha Centauri; Omega, Iota and Oop flee.

**MARTIANS AND EARTHLINGS ARE FRIENDS**

The Historian says the “dusty old” nursery-rhyme book saved Earth. Five centuries later Earth and Mars are friends; Noodle has replaced Think-Tank and Earth has established a Mars library, though Martians still fear *Mother Goose*.

### Exam-worthy imagery, irony & symbols

- **Visual metaphor / humour:** Think-Tank’s huge egg-shaped head is called a “balloon brain”; he boasts of his intelligence while repeatedly misunderstanding books.
- **Verbal irony / wordplay:** Think-Tank asks his mirror who is most intelligent, and it answers “You, sir”; Noodle’s excessive flattery exposes the ruler’s vanity.
- **Comic misunderstanding:** Martians mistake books for hats, sandwiches, then communication devices; nursery-rhyme images of “silver,” “shells” and “garden” are misread as advanced technology.
- **Situational irony / allusion:** A harmless Mother Goose book stops the invasion. Humpty Dumpty’s egg-shaped image resembles Think-Tank, frightening him into fleeing Mars.
