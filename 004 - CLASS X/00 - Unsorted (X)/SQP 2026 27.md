#### 10. Answer any one of the following two questions, in about 100-120 words. ($1 \times 6 = 6$ Marks)
*(Content – 3 | Expression – 2 | Accuracy – 1)*

**A.**
* Both Valli in ‘Madam Rides the Bus’ and the young seagull in ‘His First Flight’ are protagonists who undergo significant personal transformation.  
* However, they undergo transformation through contrasting catalysts. The seagull's change is driven by acute biological necessity—prolonged hunger and family isolation force instinctive action, overriding fear. His catalyst is external and involuntary: starvation compels the desperate dive, revealing dormant flying ability.  
* Unlike the seagull's straightforward hunger, Valli's motivation includes curiosity and yearning for experience. She has observed the bus with fascination, longing for the independence and an opportunity to discover it represents. This internal yearning—for dignity, independence, and experience beyond prescribed domestic limits—acts as a catalyst in her transformation.  
* However, both catalysts function identically: forcing protagonists towards liberating suppressed potential, and demonstrating that transformation requires catalysts powerful enough to overcome fear and unwillingness, whether biological desperation or independence.  

**OR**

**B.**
* Both poems show that important learning can happen instantly, not slowly over time. When the ball falls into the water, the boy understands loss's permanence quickly. When snow falls on the speaker, the mood shift happens immediately. In both poems, one powerful moment seems to teach everything at once.  
* Berryman suggests the boy will remember this moment's lesson forever. One lost ball teaches him about loss, and responsibility—lessons that shape the rest of his life. Frost's speaker also shows lasting change: the dust of snow doesn't just make them feel better for a few minutes; it transforms how they see their day. The moment is quick, but its effect lasts.  
* The boy in ‘The Ball Poem’ doesn't think through loss step-by-step; he feels it deeply in one moment. The speaker in ‘Dust of Snow’ doesn't reason about beauty; they experience it directly.

**A.**
* The balcony symbolises the power of narrative to create reality. It exists only in language—in Ausable's detailed description and Max's belief—yet it becomes more ‘real’ than the room itself because characters act upon it. Ausable constructs the balcony's existence through plausible details: explaining the building's history, describing access routes, mentioning previous intrusions.

* Ultimately, the balcony represents the triumph of storytelling over material facts. Ausable understands that controlling the narrative gives absolute power: the fictional balcony becomes more consequential than any physical barrier.  

**OR**

**B.**
* Think-Tank represents the danger of absolute power combined with absolute ignorance. Think-Tank's refusal to admit errors transforms his foolishness into catastrophe. Throughout the play, he mistakes books for sandwiches, then hats, then weapons—yet insists each misinterpretation proves his genius. When corrected, he claims he knew it and is never wrong. This pattern reveals that his intelligence becomes useless without humility.  
* His absolute authority amplifies this flaw dangerously. As no one challenges him, Think-Tank never receives honest feedback. His space crew's fearful obedience prevents reality-check. Consequently, his errors escalate: nursery rhymes become military threats, and paranoia replaces reasoning.  
* By the climax, these combined flaws—arrogance plus refusal to acknowledge mistakes—drive catastrophic decisions. Seeing Humpty Dumpty's image, he panics completely, ordering Mars's evacuation. Think-Tank's character demonstrates that unchecked power combined with arrogance and foolishness creates destructive leadership. His massive brain becomes a liability rather than an asset.
