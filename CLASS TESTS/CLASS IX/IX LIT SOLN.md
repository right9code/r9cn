##### Section B: Short Answer Questions (6 Marks)

**Q3. Answer any two:**

**1. What comparison does Charles Swain draw between words and summer birds in the poem *Words*?**
Charles Swain compares words to summer birds to illustrate how both can be plentiful, pleasant-sounding, and easy to produce during fair weather, but ultimately superficial and unreliable. Just as summer birds flock and chatter when conditions are comfortable but disappear when seasons change, words spoken without sincerity are abundant but meaningless. The comparison highlights the poet's central message: the volume of words does not equal their value; genuine feeling and truth are what give words real weight.

**2. What differentiates mere dreamers from actual achievers, according to Ming's mother in *Follow That Dream*?**
According to Ming's mother, the key difference between dreamers and achievers is sustained, disciplined action over time. Dreamers may have vision and enthusiasm but lack the consistent effort and sacrifice required to turn those dreams into reality. Achievers, by contrast, back their dreams with years of rigorous practice, perseverance through failure, and a willingness to give up comfort for mastery. Ming's mother emphasises that world-class success demands at least a decade of focused dedication — a level of commitment that separates those who merely wish from those who actually achieve.

**3. Why does the poet in *Believe in Yourself* state that "The first step is the hardest"?**
The poet states this because beginning any challenging journey requires overcoming inertia, self-doubt, and fear of failure — all of which are strongest at the very start. Before any effort is made, the mind imagines every obstacle and worst-case outcome, making the initial action feel daunting. Once the first step is taken, however, momentum builds and the path forward becomes clearer. The line emphasises that the decision to begin — to move from intention to action — is often the most psychologically demanding part of growth and self-improvement.

##### Section C: Detailed Questions & Answers (6 Marks)

**Q5. Answer any two:**

**1. Explain how the poet uses antithesis in *Words* to contrast sincerity with verbosity.**
Charles Swain employs antithesis — the juxtaposition of contrasting ideas — throughout *Words* to sharply distinguish between authentic expression and empty chatter. He places genuine, heartfelt words against hollow, overwrought speech, suggesting that a single honest word outweighs a flood of meaningless ones. The comparison of words to weeds versus words to seeds is itself antithetical: weeds grow abundantly but serve no purpose, while seeds, though small and few, bear fruit. Similarly, words compared to summer birds (cheerful but fleeting) are set against the implied ideal of words that carry warmth through all seasons. This technique drives home the poem's core argument — that true communication is measured not by quantity but by sincerity. The antithesis forces the reader to evaluate the quality, not just the presence, of language.

**2. How does Ming's mother balance encouragement with caution when advising her daughter in *Follow That Dream*?**
Ming's mother exemplifies the wisdom of a mentor who neither crushes aspiration nor enables naive overconfidence. She encourages Ming to pursue her dream of becoming a world-class designer, validating her passion and potential. However, she tempers this encouragement with a realistic warning: that dreams without a decade of dedicated, disciplined work remain dreams. She does not discourage Ming from dreaming — she enlarges the dream by explaining what it truly demands. This balance is important because it protects Ming from the disillusionment that comes from expecting success without effort, while still nurturing her motivation. Her advice is simultaneously affirming and grounding, pushing Ming toward both ambition and accountability.

**3. How does the poem *Believe in Yourself* use antithesis and contrast (such as comfort vs. growth) to deliver its motivational message?**
*Believe in Yourself* is structured around a series of contrasts that collectively argue for the necessity of risk and courage in personal development. The most prominent contrast is between the comfort zone — safe, familiar, unchallenging — and genuine growth, which can only happen in the unfamiliar and difficult. The poet implicitly contrasts stagnation with progress, fear with action, and self-doubt with self-belief. By presenting comfort as the obstacle rather than the goal, the poem reframes ease as a kind of trap and struggle as a form of progress. The antithetical structure keeps the reader alert to the choice being made at every moment: to stay or to dare. The motivational message is reinforced precisely because what is comfortable is shown to be what holds us back, while what is difficult is shown to be what sets us free.
