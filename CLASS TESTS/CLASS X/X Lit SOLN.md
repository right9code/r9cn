**Q2. Why did Custard keep crying for a "nice safe cage"? Did his physical appearance match his timid behaviour?**

Custard cried for a safe cage because despite his fearsome, dragon-like appearance — sharp spikes, big teeth, and fire-breathing capability — he was deeply timid and cowardly by nature. His behaviour did not match his appearance at all; while the others were outwardly small and soft yet boasted of bravery, Custard was physically formidable but inwardly fearful. This irony — the monstrous-looking creature craving safety — is the poem's central comic device, highlighting that appearances can be deeply misleading.

**Q3. What profound truth did Kisa Gotami realize when she sat at the wayside watching the flickering city lights?**

Sitting at the wayside, Kisa Gotami gazed at the flickering city lights and realised a profound truth about the impermanence of human life. Just as lamps flare up, burn for a time, and are inevitably extinguished, so too are human lives — they are lit briefly, burn with warmth and vitality, and then go out. Death is not an exception or an injustice; it is the universal, inescapable condition of all living beings. This realisation helped her accept her son's death and move toward peace rather than prolonged grief.

**Q4. Why does Anne Gregory say she can dye her hair brown, black, or carrot? What point does she want to prove?**

Anne Gregory says she can change her hair colour to prove that physical appearance — including her striking yellow hair — is mutable, not fixed or essential. Her point is that if a man loves her only for her golden hair, that love is shallow and circumstantial. True love, she argues, should be directed at the inner self — who she *is*, not what she looks like — because external beauty can always be altered. She challenges the notion that anyone can truly love another person purely for reasons beyond physical attraction.

**Q5. (A) "Courage is spontaneous, while claims of bravery can be hollow." Contrast the reactions of Belinda's pets during peace versus when the pirate attacked.**

In times of peace, Belinda's pets — Ink the cat, Blink the mouse, Mustard the yellow dog, and Custard the dragon — each present themselves in a particular light. Ink, Blink, and Mustard proudly boast of their bravery and courage, mocking poor Custard, who openly admits his timidity and begs for a safe cage. Their confident posturing during peaceful times creates an impression of fearlessness and valor.

However, when the pirate suddenly attacks, the reality is revealed with sharp irony. Ink, Blink, and Mustard immediately flee in terror — the cat hides, the mouse scurries away, and even the supposedly brave dog whimpers and seeks refuge. All their boasted courage evaporates in the face of actual danger.

Custard, by contrast — the one everyone mocked as a coward — displays remarkable, spontaneous bravery. He charges at the pirate ferociously, gobbles him up, and saves everyone. His courage was not announced in advance; it emerged instinctively when it was truly needed.

The poem thus delivers a powerful lesson: real courage is not the loudest voice in peacetime but the steadiest heart in a crisis. Genuine bravery is demonstrated through action, not proclamation.

**Q5. (B) How does the Buddha use the metaphors of ripe fruits and earthen vessels to explain human mortality? How can one overcome grief and attain peace of mind?**

In the *Sermon at Benares*, the Buddha employs two simple yet deeply resonant metaphors to help Kisa Gotami — and through her, all listeners — understand the inevitability of death. The metaphor of ripe fruits conveys that just as ripe fruits on a tree are always at risk of falling — they do not fall because of any external wrongdoing but simply because that is their nature — so too are humans always susceptible to death from the moment of birth. No one is exempt; no one's life is more protected than a fruit clinging to its branch.

The metaphor of earthen vessels reinforces this idea. Earthen pots, no matter how carefully made or lovingly used, are fragile and will eventually break. Human beings are similarly fragile — mortal by nature, not by fault or misfortune.

Through these metaphors, the Buddha teaches that grief arises from the false belief that death is an injustice or an aberration. Once one accepts that mortality is the universal condition of all beings, the sting of loss loses its power to overwhelm.

To overcome grief and attain peace, the Buddha advises: stop seeking the impossible (like a mustard seed from a household untouched by death), accept impermanence, and direct one's energy toward the living rather than the lost. Peace comes from understanding — not denial — and from releasing the selfish aspects of mourning that arise from attachment.
