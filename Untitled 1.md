# CHAPTER 06: CONDITIONAL  (TYPE 1 & SYSTEMIC OVERVIEW)

---

## 1. Theoretical Foundation: The Conditional Architecture

A **Conditional Sentence** is a complex grammatical structure composed of two interdependent clauses: a condition (the premise) and a consequence (the result). 

$$\text{Conditional Sentence} = \text{Conditional Clause (Protasis)} + \text{Main Clause (Apodosis)}$$

1. **The Conditional Clause (Subordinate / *If*-Clause / Protasis):**  
   Establishes the prerequisite, circumstance, or hypothesis under which an action can occur. It cannot stand alone as an independent syntactic thought.
2. **The Main Clause (Principal / Result Clause / Apodosis):**  
   Expresses the outcome, consequence, or deduction that is realized upon the fulfillment of the condition stated in the subordinate clause.

---

## 2. Comparative Matrix: The Four Standard Conditionals

Before examining **Type 1** in detail, study where it stands within the broader English conditional system:

| Conditional Type | Temporal Orientation | Epistemic Reality / Likelihood | Verb Form in *If*-Clause | Verb Form in Main Clause | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Zero** | Atemporal / Omnipresent | Universal truth, physical law, habitual certainty | Simple Present ($V_1 / -s / -es$) | Simple Present ($V_1 / -s / -es$) | *If you heat ice, it melts.* |
| **Type 1 (First)** | Present / Future | **Real, open, or probable future event** | **Simple Present ($V_1$) / Present Perf. / Pres. Cont.** | **will / shall / can / may / must + Base Verb ($V_1$)** | *If it rains, we will cancel the match.* |
| **Type 2 (Second)** | Present / Future | Unreal, hypothetical, imaginary, contrary to fact | Simple Past ($V_2$ / *were*) | would / could / might + Base Verb ($V_1$) | *If I won the lottery, I would buy an island.* |
| **Type 3 (Third)** | Past | Impossible, unfulfilled historical condition, regret | Past Perfect (*had* + $V_3$) | would / could / might + *have* + $V_3$ | *If you had studied, you would have passed.* |

---

## 3. Deep Dive: Conditional Clause Type 1 (The Open / Real Conditional)

### 3.1 Definition & Semantic Scope
Type 1 conditionals indicate that an event in the present or future is **likely, feasible, or open**. Unlike Type 2 (which is counterfactual or imaginary), Type 1 implies that the condition is genuinely possible:
* *If the flight is delayed, the airline will provide accommodation.* (The delay is an actual operational possibility).

### 3.2 Canonical Sentence Formula

$$\mathbf{If + \text{Subject} + \text{Verb (Simple Present)}, \quad \text{Subject} + \left\{\begin{matrix} \text{will} / \text{shall} \\ \text{can} / \text{may} \\ \text{must} / \text{should} \end{matrix}\right\} + V_1\ (\text{Base Form})}$$

### 3.3 Clause Order and Strict Punctuation Protocols
* **Subordinate Clause First:** When the *if*-clause introduces the sentence, a **comma** must separate it from the main clause.
  $$\mathbf{If\text{-clause}\ [,] \longrightarrow \text{Main Clause}}$$
  * *Example:* **If the supply chain breaks down,** consumer prices will rise sharply.
* **Main Clause First:** When the principal clause introduces the sentence, **no comma** is placed before the subordinate conjunction.
  $$\mathbf{\text{Main Clause}\ [\varnothing] \longrightarrow If\text{-clause}}$$
  * *Example:* Consumer prices will rise sharply **if** the supply chain breaks down.

---

### 3.4 Modal Variations in the Main Clause (Apodosis)

The main clause is not restricted solely to *will*. The choice of modal auxiliary modifies the epistemic force (certainty, permission, obligation, possibility) of the outcome:

```
                                  MAIN CLAUSE MODAL VARIANTS
                                               │
    ┌──────────────────┬───────────────────────┼──────────────────────┬──────────────────┐
    │                  │                       │                      │                  │
Certainty/Prediction  Ability/Permission   Possibility/Doubt      Duty/Advice        Command/Request
  (will / shall)         (can / could)       (may / might)      (must / should)       (Imperative)
```

1. **Prediction / High Certainty (*will / shall*):**
   * *If the budget is approved, construction **will commence** next Monday.*
2. **Ability or Potential (*can*):**
   * *If you clear the preliminary exam, you **can sit** for the mains.*
3. **Formal Permission (*may / can*):**
   * *If the proctor inspects your hall ticket, you **may enter** the examination hall.*
4. **Possibility / Reduced Certainty (*may / might*):**
   * *If the skies clear, we **might observe** the lunar eclipse tonight.* (Remote possibility)
5. **Necessity, Compulsion, or Urgent Advice (*must / should / ought to*):**
   * *If you develop symptoms of dehydration, you **must consume** electrolytes immediately.*
   * *If you want to maintain cardiovascular health, you **should exercise** daily.*
6. **Direct Imperative (Commands, Instructions, Invitations):**
   * Structural formula: $\mathbf{If + \text{Simple Present}, \quad V_1\ (\text{Base Verb}) + \text{Object/Modifier}}$
   * *If an earthquake strikes, **drop, cover, and hold on**.*
   * *If you meet Dr. Watson, **deliver** this dossier to him.*

---

### 3.5 Tense Variations Within the Subordinate Clause (Protasis)

While the Simple Present is the default tense in the *if*-clause, English allows aspectual shifts depending on the specific temporal logic required:

#### A. Present Continuous in the *If*-Clause
Used when the condition is an **activity currently in progress** or an **imminent, planned action**:
* *If you **are searching** for the key, it will be in the top drawer.*
* *If they **are arriving** on the 6:00 PM train, we will dispatch a car now.*

#### B. Present Perfect in the *If*-Clause
Used when the condition requires that an action be **fully completed** before the consequence can occur:
* *If you **have completed** the experiment, you may leave the laboratory.*
* *Unless she **has signed** the non-disclosure agreement, we will not share the source code.*

#### C. The Modal Auxiliary *Should* in the *If*-Clause (Remote/Contingent Possibility)
Inserting *should* into an *if*-clause indicates that the speaker regards the condition as possible, but relatively unlikely:
* *If you **should encounter** any discrepancy in the ledger, please notify the auditor.*

---

### 3.6 Advanced Inversion: Omitting *If* in Type 1 Conditionals

In formal, literary, or legal English, conditional sentences using *should* can undergo **Subject-Auxiliary Inversion**, entirely eliminating the conjunction *if*:

$$\mathbf{If + \text{Subject} + should + V_1 \implies Should + \text{Subject} + V_1}$$

* **Standard:** *If you should require additional funding, the bank will review your application.*
* **Inverted (Formal):** ***Should you require** additional funding, the bank will review your application.*
* **Negative Inversion:** ***Should you not receive** the OTP within sixty seconds, click 'Resend'.* (Do not use *should you don't receive*).

---

### 3.7 Exceptional Uses: *Will / Would* Inside the *If*-Clause

Standard grammar rules warn that *will* must never appear in an *if*-clause. However, there are two advanced, legitimate exceptions to this rule:

1. **Polite Request / Willingness:** When *will* is used not as a future marker, but as a volitional modal meaning *"be willing to"*:
   * *If you **will wait** here for a moment, I will call the manager.* (= If you are kind enough / willing to wait)
2. **Obstinate Insistence / Habitual Refusal:** When *will* indicates stubborn persistence:
   * *If you **will eat** unhygienic street food every day, you will fall ill.* (= If you stubbornly insist on eating)

---

## 4. Alternative Conditional Connectors

English offers a rich spectrum of subordinating conjunctions beyond the word *if*:

```
                               ALTERNATIVE CONNECTORS
                                         │
    ┌───────────────────────┬────────────┴──────────────┬─────────────────────────┐
    │                       │                           │                         │
 Negative Condition     Precautionary               Contractual               Hypothetical
   (Unless)               (In case)        (Provided that / As long as)     (Suppose / Supposing)
```

### 1. UNLESS ($\equiv \text{If} \dots \text{not}$)
* Expresses an exception. *Unless* carries inherent negative polarity.
* **Golden Rule:** The clause immediately following *unless* must contain an **affirmative verb**. Do not insert an auxiliary negative (*do not / did not*).
  * *Incorrect:* $\times$ *Unless you do not submit the dossier, you will be penalized.*
  * *Correct:* $\checkmark$ *Unless you submit the dossier, you will be penalized.*
  * *Paraphrase:* *If you do not submit the dossier, you will be penalized.*

### 2. PROVIDED THAT / PROVIDING THAT / ON CONDITION THAT
* Used to introduce a restrictive, binding prerequisite (common in legal, contractual, and technical prose):
  * *You will receive the grant **provided that** your research methodology meets ethical standards.*

### 3. AS LONG AS / SO LONG AS
* Emphasizes the duration or continuity of the required condition:
  * ***As long as** you adhere to the terms of the agreement, no legal action will be initiated.*

### 4. IN CASE vs. IF (Critical Distinction)
* **If** denotes that an action will happen *only after* the condition occurs.
* **In case** denotes a **precautionary measure taken beforehand** in anticipation of a potential event.
  * *I will buy an umbrella **if** it rains.* (Purchase occurs *after/if* rain begins).
  * *I will carry an umbrella **in case** it rains.* (Carrying happens *beforehand*, as a safeguard).

### 5. SUPPOSE / SUPPOSING (THAT)
* Often introduces an open conditional premise in conversational or interrogative rhetoric:
  * ***Supposing** the flight gets cancelled, what will we do?*

---

## 5. Comprehensive Diagnostic Matrix: Common Errors

| Error Category | Erroneous Formulation | Corrected Formulation | Grammatical Rationale |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Future Tense in Protasis** | $\times$ If the train **will arrive** late, we will miss the event. | $\checkmark$ If the train **arrives** late, we will miss the event. | Subordinate time/condition clauses use the Simple Present to indicate future events. |
| **Double Negative with *Unless*** | $\times$ Unless you **do not practice**, you will not improve. | $\checkmark$ Unless you **practice**, you will not improve. | *Unless* is inherently negative; combining it with *not* causes an unintended double negative. |
| **Subject-Verb Discord** | $\times$ If he **go** to London, he will visit the museum. | $\checkmark$ If he **goes** to London, he will visit the museum. | The third-person singular subject (*he/she/it*) requires $V_1 + -s/-es$. |
| **Erroneous Comma Splice** | $\times$ The plant will flourish, if it receives sunlight. | $\checkmark$ The plant will flourish **if** it receives sunlight. | No comma is permitted when the main clause precedes the subordinate conditional clause. |
| **Faulty Inversion Syntax** | $\times$ Should you **will need** help, let me know. | $\checkmark$ **Should you need** help, let me know. | Inversion with *should* requires the base verb ($V_1$), not another modal auxiliary. |

# CHAPTER 07: CLAUSES — NOUN CLAUSE AND RELATIVE (ADJECTIVE) CLAUSE

---

## 1. Syntactic Architecture: Phrase vs. Clause vs. Sentence

```
  SYNTACTIC UNIT       CONSTITUENT PROPERTIES                               EXAMPLE
┌────────────────┐  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐  ┌───────────────────────────┐
│     PHRASE     │  │ Group of words; lacks a Subject + Finite Verb.   │  │ "in the dead of night"    │
└────────────────┘  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘  └───────────────────────────┘
        │
        ▼
┌────────────────┐  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐  ┌───────────────────────────┐
│     CLAUSE     │  │ Contains a Subject + Finite Verb; can be         │  │ "because the alarm rang"  │
│                │  │ independent or dependent (subordinate).          │  │ (Dependent Clause)        │
└────────────────┘  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘  └───────────────────────────┘
        │
        ▼
┌────────────────┐  ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐  ┌───────────────────────────┐
│    SENTENCE    │  │ Contains at least one independent clause;        │  │ "The alarm rang loudly."  │
│                │  │ expresses a complete, standalone thought.        │  │                           │
└────────────────┘  └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘  └───────────────────────────┘
```

A **Finite Verb** is a verb form marked for person, number, and tense (e.g., *writes, wrote, is running*). Non-finite forms (infinitives: *to write*, gerunds/participles: *writing, written*) cannot anchor a clause on their own.

---

## 2. Structural Taxonomy of Subordinate Clauses

Subordinate clauses perform the functional role of a single part of speech within a complex sentence:

```
                          SUBORDINATE (DEPENDENT) CLAUSES
                                         │
        ┌────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┐
        │                                │                                │
   NOUN CLAUSE                     RELATIVE CLAUSE                  ADVERB CLAUSE
Performs the nominal role         Performs the adjectival role      Performs the adverbial role
(Subject, Object, Complement)     (Modifies an antecedent noun)     (Modifies Verb/Adj/Adv for
                                                                     Time, Cause, Condition, etc.)
```

---

## 3. PART 1: The Noun Clause

### 3.1 Definition & Nature
A **Noun Clause** is a subordinate clause that fulfills the exact syntactic role of a **noun** or **noun phrase**. Because it behaves as a nominal entity, it can serve as a subject, object, or complement.

$$\text{Diagnostic Question:} \quad \mathbf{\text{Subject}} + \mathbf{\text{Main Verb}} + \mathbf{\text{[WHAT? / WHOM?]}} \implies \mathbf{\text{Noun Clause}}$$

* *Sentence:* The archeologist announced **that a new tomb had been discovered**.  
  *Test:* Announced *what?* $\rightarrow$ **that a new tomb had been discovered** (Noun Clause).

### 3.2 Formal Connectors Introducing Noun Clauses
1. **The Complementizer:** `that` (Often omitted in informal speech, mandatory in formal registers).
2. **Interrogative Dubitative Conjunctions:** `if`, `whether` (Expressing doubt, choice, or condition).
3. **Interrogative Pronouns / Determiners / Adverbs:** `who`, `whom`, `whose`, `what`, `which`, `where`, `when`, `why`, `how`.
4. **Indefinite Compound Relatives:** `whoever`, `whomever`, `whichever`, `whatever`.

---

### 3.3 The Five Core Syntactic Functions of Noun Clauses

```
                           SYNTACTIC FUNCTIONS OF A NOUN CLAUSE
                                             │
      ┌──────────────────┬───────────────────┼───────────────────┬──────────────────┐
      │                  │                   │                   │                  │
 1. Subject of Verb  2. Object of Verb  3. Prepositional Obj   4. Subject Compl   5. Apposition
```

#### Function 1: Subject of a Finite Verb
The entire clause occupies the subject slot directly preceding the predicate verb:
* [**What the whistleblower disclosed**] *shocked* the entire nation.
* [**That air travel is significantly safer than road transport**] *is* an indisputable statistical fact.
* [**Whether the summit will yield peace**] *remains* uncertain.

#### Function 2: Direct Object of a Transitive Verb
The clause receives the action of a transitive verb, answering the query *What?* or *Whom?*:
* The meteorologist predicts [**that a tropical storm will make landfall by midnight**].
* We cannot determine [**who authorized this expenditure**].
* I do not know [**whether she accepted the scholarship**].

#### Function 3: Object of a Preposition
The clause directly follows a preposition, completing the prepositional phrase:
* The verdict will depend heavily on [**how the jury interprets the forensic evidence**].
* Pay close attention to [**what the instructor demonstrates**].
* He took pride in [**that his team maintained academic integrity**].

#### Function 4: Subject Complement (Predicate Noun)
The clause follows a linking / copular verb (*is, was, were, become, seems, appears*) and renames or defines the subject:
* The truth is [**that no contingency plan was prepared**].
* My primary concern remains [**how we will finance the deficit**].
* Success is [**what you make of your opportunities**].

#### Function 5: Apposition to an Abstract Noun
The clause stands beside an abstract noun (such as *fact, news, rumor, report, idea, allegation, belief, discovery*) to explain, define, or expand its specific content:
* The rumor [**that the factory was shutting down**] caused mass panic.
* We cannot overlook the fact [**that the accused has an ironclad alibi**].

> **Crucial Apposition Check:** In an appositive noun clause introduced by *that*, the word *that* is a pure conjunction, not a relative pronoun. The clause following *that* must be **grammatically complete on its own**:
> * *The report that the economy is stabilizing is accurate.* $\rightarrow$ (*"the economy is stabilizing"* is a complete clause; hence, apposition).

---

### 3.4 The Indirect Question Constraint (Embedded Questions)

When an interrogative clause becomes a noun clause within a declarative sentence, it loses its direct question form. **Subject-auxiliary inversion is strictly forbidden, and no auxiliary do/does/did may be inserted.**

$$\mathbf{\text{Direct: Wh-} + \text{Auxiliary} + \text{Subject} + \text{Verb} \implies \text{Noun Clause: Wh-} + \text{Subject} + \text{Verb}}$$

* *Direct Question:* Where **does he live**?
* $\times$ *Erroneous:* I wonder where **does he live**.
* $\checkmark$ *Correct:* I wonder where **he lives**.
* *Direct Question:* Why **did the machine fail**?
* $\times$ *Erroneous:* The engineer explained why **did the machine fail**.
* $\checkmark$ *Correct:* The engineer explained why **the machine failed**.

---

## 4. PART 2: The Relative (Adjective) Clause

### 4.1 Definition & Mechanism
A **Relative Clause** is a subordinate clause that modifies, qualifies, or describes a **noun or pronoun** in the main clause. The noun or pronoun being modified is called the **Antecedent**.

$$\text{Diagnostic Question:} \quad \mathbf{\text{Antecedent Noun}} + \mathbf{\text{[WHICH ONE? / WHAT KIND OF? / WHOSE?]}} \implies \mathbf{\text{Relative Clause}}$$

* *Sentence:* The neurosurgeon [**who performed the critical operation**] trained in Zurich.  
  *Test:* Which neurosurgeon? $\rightarrow$ *who performed the critical operation* (Relative Clause modifying *neurosurgeon*).

---

### 4.2 Matrix of Relative Pronouns and Relative Adverbs

| Relative Word | Grammatical Category | Syntactic Function within Relative Clause | Antecedent Entity | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Who** | Relative Pronoun | Subject | Persons | The artisan **who crafted this violin** is blind. |
| **Whom** | Relative Pronoun | Direct / Prepositional Object | Persons (Formal register) | The ambassador **whom the Prime Minister appointed** has arrived. |
| **Whose** | Relative Pronoun | Possessive Determiner | Persons, Animals, Inanimate objects | The researcher **whose paper was cited** won the prize. |
| **Which** | Relative Pronoun | Subject or Object | Animals, Inanimate Objects, Ideas | The manuscript **which was lost** has been recovered. |
| **That** | Relative Pronoun | Subject or Object (Restrictive only) | Persons, Animals, Inanimate Entities | The bridge **that connects the islands** collapsed. |
| **Where** | Relative Adverb | Adverbial modifier of Place | Geographical / Spatial Nouns | The village **where the treaty was signed** is remote. |
| **When** | Relative Adverb | Adverbial modifier of Time | Temporal Nouns (*day, era, moment*) | I recall the hour **when the declaration was broadcast**. |
| **Why** | Relative Adverb | Adverbial modifier of Reason | Reason Nouns (*reason, rationale*) | The reason **why the proposal fell through** is obvious. |

---

### 4.3 Defining (Restrictive) vs. Non-Defining (Non-Restrictive) Relative Clauses

This distinction is central to English syntax and punctuation:

```
                            RELATIVE CLAUSE DICHOTOMY
                                        │
           ┌────────────────────────────┴────────────────────────────┐
           ▼                                                         ▼
  DEFINING (RESTRICTIVE)                                  NON-DEFINING (NON-RESTRICTIVE)
- Identifies WHICH specific entity is meant.              - Adds parenthetical, non-essential detail.
- Essential to the basic meaning of the sentence.         - Can be deleted without altering identification.
- NO COMMAS permitted.                                   - MUST be separated by COMMAS (, ... ,).
- Can use "that" instead of "which" or "who".             - CANNOT use "that" under any circumstance.
```

#### Comparative Case Study:
* **Defining:** *The passengers **who fastened their seatbelts** escaped injury.*  
  *Meaning:* Only that specific subset of passengers who fastened their belts remained uninjured. The rest were hurt.
* **Non-Defining:** *The passengers**, who fastened their seatbelts,** escaped injury.*  
  *Meaning:* All the passengers escaped injury; as an additional, parenthetical detail, they happened to have fastened their belts.

---

### 4.4 Advanced Rules Governing the Relative Pronoun *That*

In high-level standard English, the relative pronoun **that** is preferred or required over *which* or *who* under specific grammatical conditions:

1. **Following Superlative Adjectives:**
   * *This is the **most intricate** mechanism **that** has ever been engineered.* (Not *which*).
2. **Following Absolute Indefinite Pronouns (*all, any, none, only, little, much, everything, nothing*):**
   * *Man is the only mammal **that** possesses articulate speech.*
   * *All **that** glitters is not gold.* (Not *All which...*).
   * *There is nothing **that** can console him now.*
3. **Following Compound Antecedents (Person + Animal / Inanimate Object):**
   * *The shepherd and his flock **that** were caught in the blizzard have perished.* (Neither *who* nor *which* can agree with both).

---

### 4.5 The Contact Clause (Zero Relative Pronoun / Omission)
When a relative pronoun serves as the **object** of a defining relative clause, it may be omitted entirely:
* *The monograph **which** you recommended was illuminating.*
* $\implies$ *The monograph **[ $\varnothing$ ]** you recommended was illuminating.* (Contact Clause).

> **Syntax Warning:** A relative pronoun functioning as the **subject** of its clause can **never** be omitted:
> * $\times$ *The woman spoke at the seminar was an economist.*
> * $\checkmark$ *The woman **who** spoke at the seminar was an economist.*

---

### 4.6 Preposition Placement: Stranding vs. Pied-Piping
* **Informal / Conversational (Preposition Stranding):** The preposition remains at the end of the relative clause:
  * *The firm [which she worked **for**] has declared bankruptcy.*
* **Formal / Academic (Pied-Piping):** The preposition moves before the relative pronoun:
  * *The firm [**for which** she worked] has declared bankruptcy.*
  * Note: You cannot use *that* after a fronted preposition: $\times$ *The firm for that she worked...* (Unacceptable).

---

### 4.7 The Sentential Relative Clause
A sentential relative clause does not modify a single noun. Instead, it **modifies the entire proposition of the preceding independent clause**. It is always non-defining, preceded by a comma, and must use **which**:
* *The dam cracked during the monsoon**, **which necessitated the evacuation of twenty villages**.*  
  *(Here, 'which' refers to the entire event of the dam cracking).*

---

## 5. Diagnostic Disambiguation: Noun Clause vs. Relative Clause

Because words like *that, where, when, who* can introduce both types of clauses, rely on this analytical test:

```
                                 DISAMBIGUATION PROTOCOL
                                            │
                     Examine the word immediately preceding the clause
                                            │
                    ┌───────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
                    │                                               │
             VERB or PREPOSITION                              NOUN or PRONOUN
                    │                                               │
                    ▼                                               ▼
      Does it answer "WHAT?" or "WHOM?"              Does it answer "WHICH ONE?" / "WHAT KIND?"
                    │                                               │
                    ▼                                               ▼
               NOUN CLAUSE                                   RELATIVE CLAUSE
    "I discovered WHERE HE WAS HIDING."            "I visited the cave WHERE HE WAS HIDING."
    (Discovered what? -> Noun Clause)              (Which cave? -> Relative Clause modifying cave)
```

### Analytical Comparison:

#### Pair A: Introduced by *Where*
1. *I know [**where the treasure is buried**].*  
   $\rightarrow$ Preceded by the transitive verb *know*. Answers *know what?* $\implies$ **Noun Clause (Direct Object)**.
2. *I visited the island [**where the treasure is buried**].*  
   $\rightarrow$ Preceded by the noun *island*. Answers *which island?* $\implies$ **Relative Clause (Modifying island)**.

#### Pair B: Introduced by *That*
1. *The hypothesis [**that the universe is expanding**] is supported by red-shift observations.*  
   $\rightarrow$ The clause defines the content of *hypothesis*. The clause *the universe is expanding* is structurally complete on its own. $\implies$ **Noun Clause (In Apposition to 'hypothesis')**.
2. *The hypothesis [**that the astrophysicist proposed**] was debated worldwide.*  
   $\rightarrow$ The clause modifies *hypothesis*, and *that* functions as the direct object of *proposed* (the astrophysicist proposed [what?] $\rightarrow$ *that*). $\implies$ **Relative Clause (Modifying 'hypothesis')**.

### Exercise Set I: Verb Forms
1. **ratifies** — Type 1 conditional *if*-clause requires Simple Present. Third-person singular subject (*the board of directors*, acting as a collective single entity here) takes $V_1 + -s/-es$.
2. **overhauls** — The conjunction *unless* introduces a conditional clause requiring Simple Present. Third-person singular (*the municipal corporation*) takes *overhauls*.
3. **stand** — Simple Present base form with subject *you*.
4. **detect** — The inverted structure *Should + Subject + Base Verb* requires the uninflected bare infinitive *detect* (not *detects* or *detected*).
5. **does not reach** — Negative Simple Present for third-person singular *the courier*.
6. **will crystallize** — Main clause consequence requires *will + base verb*.
7. **revises** — *Provided that* operates identically to *if*, demanding Simple Present in the conditional clause.
8. **is** — Simple Present of the copular verb *to be* for third-person singular *the witness*.
9. **are** — Simple Present plural form of *be* for the plural subject *international sanctions*.
10. **have already finished** — Present Perfect aspect within an *if*-clause is used when an action must be completed before the consequence (*may pass*) can take place.

### Exercise Set II: Transformations
1. **Unless you register your biometric profile, you cannot access the secure server.**  
   *(The negative marker 'do not' is eliminated because 'unless' is inherently negative).*
2. **Should the foreign delegation arrive ahead of schedule, escort them directly to the conference hall.**  
   *(Inversion drops 'if' and moves the auxiliary modal 'should' to sentence-initial position).*
3. **Keep your receipt safe in case you need to exchange the merchandise later.**  
   *('In case' correctly frames the precaution taken ahead of a possible contingency).*
4. **We will sanction the enterprise loan provided that you provide collateral security.**  
   *(Or: '...provided that you furnish collateral security' to avoid lexical repetition).*
5. **If you do not hurry up, you will miss the express train.**  
   *(Alternative: 'If you hurry up, you will catch the express train').*

### Exercise Set IV: Sentence Synthesis
1. **The ancient manuscript, whose fragile parchment was damaged by floodwaters, has been restored.**  
   *(Non-Defining relative clause; enclosed in commas because the manuscript is already specified).*

   *(Non-Defining relative clause; proper nouns take non-restrictive relative clauses set off by commas).*

### Exercise Set V: Proofreading & Editing
1. **Error:** *Unless the government does not control...*  
   **Correction:** **Unless the government controls** the fiscal deficit, inflation will escalate. *(Rule: 'Unless' cannot take an auxiliary negative).*
2. **Error:** *If he will study...*  
   **Correction:** **If he studies** diligently for the civil services examination, he will clear it on his first attempt. *(Rule: Future modal 'will' is prohibited in standard conditional clauses).*
3. **Error:** *inquired that why...*  
   **Correction:** The magistrate **inquired why** the defendant had absconded from the jurisdiction of the court. *(Rule: The conjunction 'that' cannot be placed before an interrogative connector like 'why').*
4. **Error:** *William Shakespeare who wrote thirty-seven plays was born...*  
   **Correction:** William Shakespeare**, who wrote thirty-seven plays,** was born in Stratford-upon-Avon. *(Rule: Non-defining relative clauses modifying proper nouns must be enclosed in commas).*
5. **Error:** *where had the suspect concealed...*  
   **Correction:** The detective could not establish **where the suspect had concealed** the stolen bearer bonds. *(Rule: Indirect/embedded noun clauses must preserve Subject-Verb declarative order without auxiliary inversion).*
6. **Error:** *fountain pen who I purchased...*  
   **Correction:** This is the same fountain pen **that** [or: **which**] I purchased at the vintage bazaar yesterday. *(Rule: Inanimate antecedents require 'which' or 'that', never 'who'; 'that' is preferred following 'the same').*

### Exercise Set VI: Multiple Choice Questions
1. **(B) notify**  
   *Explanation:* The sentence uses an inverted conditional structure in the protasis (*Should you experience...*), while the main clause is an **Imperative clause**. Imperatives always begin with the uninflected base verb ($V_1$).
2. **(B) The solar panels that were installed on the auditorium roof generate ten kilowatts daily.**  
   *Explanation:* Option (B) is a Defining (restrictive) relative clause that identifies which panels are meant, uses *that*, and contains no commas. Options (A), (C), and (D) are non-defining relative clauses set off by commas.
3. **(B) why the ballistic analysis took so long**  
   *Explanation:* An indirect question functioning as a noun clause must follow declarative syntax (Subject + Verb: *the ballistic analysis took...*). It must not contain auxiliary inversion (*did the analysis take*) and must not prefix *that* onto *why*.
4. **(C) as long as**  
   *Explanation:* *As long as* emphasizes the ongoing contractual condition (*provided that / during the time that*). *Unless* reverses the logic, *in case* denotes an advance precaution, and *suppose* is interrogative.
5. **(B) A noun clause in apposition to 'doctrine'**  
   *Explanation:* The clause *that the earth revolves around the sun* defines the substance of the abstract noun *doctrine*. The clause following *that* is structurally complete on its own (*the earth revolves around the sun*), which proves that *that* is a conjunction introducing an appositive noun clause, not an adjectival relative pronoun.
