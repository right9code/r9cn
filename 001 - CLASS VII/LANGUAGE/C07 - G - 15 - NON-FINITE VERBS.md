# Non-finite Verbs  

Read these sets of sentences. What do you notice about the highlighted words and the underlined words?  

- I want **to go** home.  
    - Lathika wants **to go** home.
    - They want **to go** home.
- I am **swimming**.  
    - Mansi is **swimming**.
    - They are **swimming**.  

In a sentence, the verb usually changes its form according to the person and number of the subject. However, in the above sentences, the underlined words remain the same even when the subject changes.  

When the verb in the sentence indicates the tense and changes according to the subject, we call it the **finite verb**. A finite verb has a subject.  

The verb in a sentence that has no subject, person or number is called a **non-finite verb**.  

In these sentences, the highlighted verbs are finite verbs that change with the subject, while the underlined verbs are non-finite verbs.  
- I go to the market every day **to fetch** milk.  
- He goes to the market every day **to fetch** milk.  
There are three kinds of non-finite verbs—infinitives, participles and gerunds. Let us learn about them in detail.  

**Infinitives**  

Read these sentences.  
- I **want to buy** some perfume.  
- Indira **wants to buy** some clothes.  
In these sentences, the verb **want** is the **finite verb** and **to buy** is the **infinitive**.  

**Intuitives** are also called the **to-verb** because to is usually a part of the infinitive form. Let us learn about some of the functions of infinitives.  
- Infinitives can function as the subject or the object of a sentence.  
    - I want **to bathe**. **(object)**
    - **To delay** is bad. **(subject)**  

- The infinitive can be the object of the verb and also modified by an adverb.  
    - Many men prefer to work slowly.  

- An infinitive can also qualify an adjective or a noun.  
    - Apples are sweet to eat.
    - This is not the time to sleep.
**NOTE**:  
Infinitives are useful when—  
- we wish to state an aim or purpose.  
    - He called **to apologise**.
    - He wanted **to buy** milk.
    - They agreed **to help** us.
- when we wish to indicate result.  
    - She lived **to see** the outcome.
    - The news proved **to be** true.
- Infinitives can also be the object of a preposition.  
    - The teacher is about **to start**.  

Take a look at these sentences.  
- **To steal** is a sin.  
- **To eat** too much is harmful.  
These sentences are grammatically correct, but sound strange. Now, read these sentences.  
- It is a sin **to steal**.  
- It is harmful **to eat** too much.  
It is better to use it as the subject and the infinitive as the object in such sentences.  

When the **to+verb** form is used in a sentence and functions as a noun, it is called the **infinitive**.  

We usually use **to** with the infinitive. However it is not essential. When an infinitive is used without **to** it is called a bare infinitive.  
- We do not use to when we use infinitives with auxiliary verbs like **must, can, should, shall, will, may, might** and **do**. For example,  
    - I must **go** home now.
    - They might **leave** for Dehradun next week.
- We do not use to with the infinitive after verbs of perception like **see, hear, watch, know** and **feel**. For example,  
    - I saw Leena **leave** the house.
    - I **felt** an ant crawl up my leg.
- We do not use to when we use infinitives after verbs like **make, let, dare** or **need**. For example,  
    - I **made** him *speak*.
    - **Let** me *try* this.
    - How **dare** you *touch* my books?
    - You **need** not **solve** these problems.  

**NOTE**:  
With verbs like need and dare, note that we use the infinitive with to in affirmative sentences.  
Look at these sentences.  
- He **dared** to dream, and achieved his ambition.  
- I need to visit the doctor soon.

**Participles**  

You have learnt about **present participles (verb+-ing)** and **past participles 'verb+-ed/-d/-t/-en/-n)**.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- The **creaking** noise disturbed us.  
- The **leaking** pipe must be replaced.  
- The **quiverin**g leaves of the willow trees rustled in the breeze.  
The highlighted words are present participles that have been used as adjectives.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- The **injured** boy was taken to the hospital.  
- The **frightened** girl shouted loudly.  
- They **rescued** the passengers from the wrecked car.  
- Nobody ate the **burnt** toast.  
The highlighted words are past participles that have been used as adjectives.  

**NOTE**:  
We may say that a participle is a verbal adjective.  

A **participle** is that form of the verb that can act as an adjective in a sentence.  

Participles may be used—  
- as attributes. For example,  
    - The **torn** sheet flew away.
    - A **burnt** child dreads the fire.
- in the predicate, modifying the subject or the object. For example,  
    - The boy looked **terrified**.
    - He kept me **standing**.
- to talk about an earlier action. For example,  
    - **Seeing** the tiger, the man fled.
    - **Dejected** by his failure, he left his home.

We can join pairs of sentences by using participles. Look at these pairs of sentences.  
- The girl noticed a car. The car was coming towards her.  
    - The girl noticed a car **coming** towards her.
- Roma opened the cupboard. Roma took out a shawl.  
    - **Opening** the cupboard, Roma took out a shawl.
- I saw an elephant. I ran for my life.  
    - **Seeing** an elephant, I ran for my life.  

In the above examples, both actions take place at the same time. When we join the sentences, one finite verb is removed and we use its present participle form instead.  

But sometimes, the second action begins only after the first has been completed. In that case, we convert the finite verb denoting the first action into the participle.  
- He had lost all his books. He went to his friend for help.  
- **Having** lost all his books, he went to his friend for help.

**Gerunds**  
Look at these sentences. Notice how the **-ing** words have been used in these sentences.  
- **Swimming** is a good way to exercise. **(subject of the sentence)**  
- I enjoy **reading**. **(object of the sentence)**  
- He is fond of **eating** ice-cream. **(object of the preposition)**  
- What I quite abhor is **lying**. **(complement of the verb)**  
In these sentences, the **-ing** words function like nouns. However, all these words are derived from verbs.  

When the **-ing** form of the verb is used as a noun in a sentence, we call it the **gerund**.  

**NOTE**:  
A gerund is a verbal noun.  

**NOTE**:  
The -ing word can be used in different ways. For example,  
- Dancing is her hobby. (subject)  
- The girl is dancing gracefully. (verb)  
- Everybody gathered to see the dancing peacock. (adjective)

**Gerunds or Infinitives?**  
As both gerrunds and infinitives can act as nouns and verbs, they have the same uses. Thus, in many sentences either of them may be used. For example,  
- Teach me **to sing**. **(infinitive)**  
- Teach me **singing**. **(gerund)**  
- No one likes **to wait**. **(infinitive)**  
- No one likes **waiting**. **(gerund)**
