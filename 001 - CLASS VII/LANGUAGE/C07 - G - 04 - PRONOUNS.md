# Pronouns  

You have learnt that words that take the place of nouns are called **pronouns**.  

Pronouns are of different kinds.  
- personal pronouns  
- possessive pronouns  
- demonstrative pronouns  
- interrogative pronouns  
- reflexive pronouns  
- emphasising pronouns  
- indefinite pronouns  
- distributive pronouns  
- relative pronouns  

**Read this sentence.**  

- Mohit is an athlete. He practises regularly. The coach likes him.  
In the above sentence, he and him take the place of Mohit. These pronouns take the place of persons or things.  

Pronouns that take the place of people and things are called **personal pronouns**.  

We use personal pronouns depending upon—  

- **number**—singular or plural. For example,  
    - The children went to a **party**. They enjoyed **it**.
- **gender**—male, female or neuter. For example,  
    - **Mahima** is in the pool. **She** loves swimming.
    - **Tanish** is a good boy. **He** always obeys his parents.
- **person**—first person, second person or third person. For example,  
    - I told you not to tell her the secret.
    - They agreed to meet us at 8 a.m.
- **case**—subject or object. For example,  
    - Gaurav is a good student. He is also good in sports.
    - Jayant gave me the book.  

**NOTE**:  
The pronoun, **it** is used—  
- for non-living things. For example,  
    - Here is your knife. Keep it carefully.
- to refer to an earlier statement. For example,  
    - He must leave. He knows it.
- to emphasise a noun or pronoun that follows it. For example,  
- It was I who promised to help you.  

Now, read these sentences.  
    - This chair is mine.
    - Those books are theirs.
The highlighted pronouns show possession or belonging.  

Pronouns that show possession are known as **possessive pronouns**.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- **This** is a beautiful dress.  
- **Those** are ripe mangoes.  

Pronouns like **this, that, those** and **these** are used to point out certain objects.  

Pronouns that are used to point out the objects which they refer to are known as **demonstrative pronouns**.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- What would you like to have—tea or coffee?  
- Which is the way to the university?  

Pronouns like **what, who** and **which** are used to ask questions.  

Pronouns that are used to ask questions are called interrogative pronouns.  

**NOTE**:  
**This** and **these** are used to refer to things that are close by. **That** and **those** are used to refer to things that are far away.  

**NOTE**:  
**Who** is used to refer to persons. **Which** is used to refer to persons or things. **What** is used only for things.  

Read this sentence.  
- He hurt himself.  
- The cat licked itself.  

The highlighted pronouns tell us that the subject is the receiver of the action.  

The highlighted pronouns are called **reflexive pronouns** because the action of the subject reflects upon the subject itself.  

**NOTE**:  
The reflexive pronoun is used when the subject and the object of the verb refer to the same person or thing.  
A reflexive pronoun can never be the subject of a sentence.  
- My sister and myself went to Kolkata. (incorrect)  
- My sister and I went to Kolkata. (correct)  

Now, read these sentences.  
- He himself should finish the work.  
- I myself closed the door.  
The highlighted pronouns are used for emphasis.  

Pronouns that are used for the sake of emphasis are called **emphasising pronouns**.

Pronouns that refer to some person or thing in a general way and do not refer to any person in particular are called **indefinite pronouns**.  

The other indefinite pronouns are—all, another, any, anybody, anyone, anything, each, everybody, everyone, everything, few, many, nobody, none, one, several, some, somebody, someone.  

**NOTE**:  
The indefinite pronoun one, if used in a sentence should be used throughout.  
- **One** must never abandon one's duties.

Now, read these sentences.  
- **Each** of us must contribute generously.  
- **Either** of you can take down the notes.  
- **Neither** of them attended the ceremony.  
- **Either** of the two students may be sent to the competition.  
- **Neither** of the coats is suitable for the occasion.  

**NOTE**:  
**Either** and neither can be used only when speaking about two persons or things. Either means one or the other of two. **Neither** means not one nor the other of two.  

The pronouns each, either and neither refer to persons or things one at a time.  
Pronouns that refer to nouns one at a time are called **distributive pronouns**.  
Distributive pronouns are always in the singular and take singular verbs.  

Now, read this sentence.  
- The boy **who** you were talking to is the captain of the cricket team.  

In this sentence, **who** is **a relative pronoun** that refers to the noun boy that comes before. The noun is thus the **antecedent** of the pronoun. The relative pronoun should ideally be placed close to its antecedent in the sentence.  

**NOTE**:  
Generally, **who** is used while referring to people, **which** for non-living things or animals and **that** is used for people and things. As the relative pronoun refers to a noun or a pronoun, it must be of the same number and person as its antecedent. It is the verb that shows the number and person of the relative pronoun.  
Sometimes the relative pronoun is omitted. For example,  
- This is the house (**that**) my grandfather built.
