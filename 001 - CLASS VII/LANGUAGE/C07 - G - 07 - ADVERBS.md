# Adverbs  

You have learnt that an **adverb** is a word which modifies the meaning of a **verb**, an **adjective** or another **adverb**.  

**Kinds of Adverbs**  

There are six kinds of adverbs.  

**Adverbs of time** show us the time at which an action takes place.  

For example,  
- I have met Neha before.  
- The principal will meet you today.  

**Adverbs of frequency** show us how often something happens.  

For example,  
- He visits us regularly.  
- My father exercises every day.  

**Adverbs of place** show us where something happens.  

For example,  
- Sam went downstairs.  
- The man has gone out.  

**Adverbs of manner** show us how something happens.  

For example,  
- Rahim writes neatly.  
- The students worked diligently.  

**NOTE**:  
Sometimes, an adverb can modify an entire sentence. For example,  
- **Luckily**, she was not seriously injured.  

**Adverbs of degree** show the intensity or the degree of an action or a quality.  

For example,  
- I am so satisfied!  
- What you have heard is absolutely true.  

**Interrogative adverbs** are adverbs which are used at the beginning of a sentence to ask questions.  

For example,  
- When are you reaching Kolkata? **(time)**  
- Where can I meet you? **(place)**  
- How did you get here? **(manner)**  
- Why are you sad? **(reason)**

**Formation of Adverbs**  
Most adverbs of manner are formed from adjectives in the following ways.  
- Most adjectives can be changed to adverbs by adding **-ly**. For example, warm—warmly, sincere—sincerely, quiet—quietly.  
- When the adjective ends in **-y** preceded by a consonant, **-y** is changed to **-i** and **-ly** is added. For example, pretty—prettily, steady—steadily, lucky—luckily.  
- When the adjective ends in **-le**, the **-le** is changed to **-ly**. For example, possible—possibly, able—ably, gentle—gently.  
- When the adjective ends in **-ic**, we add **-ally**. For example, basic—basically, scientific—scientifically.  

**NOTE**:  
Some adverbs and adjectives have the same form, but are used differently.  
- We need fast bowlers. (adjective)  
- He bowls fast. (adverb)  
- We must be quick. (adverb)  
- The smart child gave a quick reply. (adjective)

**Position of Adverbs**  
- **Adverbs of manner**, which answer the question **How?**, are generally placed after the verb. If the verb has a direct object, then the adverb is placed after the direct object. For example,  
    - The actor is singing **loudly**.
    - He plays the flute **well**.  

**NOTE**:  
- The **direct object** is the person or thing that receives the action of the verb. The direct object follows the verb. We can identify the direct object by asking the question **Whom?** or **What?** after the verb. For example:  
  - The boy recited a poem. (What did the boy recite?)
  - Sudha is biting her nails. (What is Sudha biting?)  

- **Adverbs of place** and **adverbs of time** are also placed after the verb. If the sentence has a direct object, then the adverb is placed after the direct object. For example:  
    - It snowed **yesterday**.
    - Put the chairs **there**.
- When there are two or more adverbs after a verb, the normal order is adverb of manner followed by adverb of place and then adverb of time.  
    - Rima will come **here next month**.  

- **Adverbs of frequency**, **adverbs of degree** and sometimes **adverbs of manner** are normally put between the subject and the verb if the verb consists of only one word. For example:  
    - I quite like these shoes.
    - I always tell him to exercise.
    - She happily accepted the suggestion.  

**NOTE**:  
- If the verb consists of more than one word, then the adverb comes after the first verb. For example:  
    - The man had just entered the auditorium.
- If the verb is **am/are/is/was**, the adverbs are placed after the verb. For example:  
    - They are **always** late.
    - The rice is **just** right.
- When an adverb modifies an adjective or another adverb, the adverb usually comes before it. For example:  
    - He lifted the stone **quite** easily.
    - It is a **rather** warm day.
- The adverb **enough** is always placed after the word which it modifies. For example:  
    - Is this good **enough** for you?
    - Is the box large **enough** for all your books?
- The word **only** should be placed before the word it modifies. For example:  
    - I **only** have four bags right now.
- For emphasis, adverbs of time may also be placed at the beginning of the sentence. For example:  
    - **Tomorrow**, we shall meet.

**Use of Simple Adverbs**  
**NOTE**: the use of these common adverbs.  
- **Very** is intensively used with an adjective or another adverb in the positive degree. For example,  
    - He is a **very** nice person.
    - I typed **very** carefully.
- **Very** with a superlative is used to indicate the highest degree. For example,  
    - The coffee is of the **very** best quality.
    - I can reach by 7 a.m. at the **very** earliest.
- **Too** is used to mean more than required or excess. For example,  
    - I am **too** tired to play now.
    - It is **too** cold to go for a walk in the woods.
- **Much** is used with an adverb or an adjective in the comparative degree. For example,  
    - Things are **much** better in the city.
    - You are **much** stronger than I thought you would be.  

**NOTE**:  
We say, It is **very** hot today.  
We do not say, It is **too** hot today.  

We say, It is **too** hot to go cycling.  
We do not say, It is **very** hot to go cycling.

**One Word for Several Words**  
We often use an adverb in place of a group of words. So adverbs are useful when we wish to express something concisely. For example,  
- I reminded Ravi **again and again** that he had to pick up his sister from school.  
- I reminded Ravi **repeatedly** that he had to pick up his sister from school.
