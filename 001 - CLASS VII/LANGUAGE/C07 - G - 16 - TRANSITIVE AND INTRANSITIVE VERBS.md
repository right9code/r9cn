# Transitive and Intransitive Verbs  

We know that a sentence will always have a verb. A verb tells us—  
- what a person or thing does—He **repairs** cars.  
- what a person has—The girl **has** a pretty frock.  
- what a person or thing is—The ostrich **is** a huge bird.  

Now, look at this sentence.  
- My mother **cut** the cake.  
In this sentence, the cake receives the action of the verb.  

The person or thing that receives the action of the verb is called the **object**.  

When a verb has an object that receives the action of the verb, it is called a **transitive verb**.  

Now, read this sentence.  
- The child **wept** loudly.  
In this sentence, there is no object. No one receives the action of the **verb**.  

When a verb has no object, it is called an **intransitive verb**.  

Read these sentences.  
- My father gave me a fruit.  
- My father gave a fruit to me.  

Answer these questions.  
- Who is the doer of the action? `____` (This is the subject.)  
- What did Father give? `____` (This is the direct object.)  
- To whom did Father give? `____` (This is the indirect object.)  

Most transitive verbs take a single object. But some transitive verbs like **give, ask, promise, tell,** etc. take two objects—**a direct object** and **an indirect object**. In the above sentences, the direct object is **fruit**, while the indirect object is **me**.  

The person or thing that receives the action of the verb is called the **direct object**.  

The person or thing to whom or on whom the action of the verb is done or who receives the direct object is called the **indirect object**.  

**NOTE**:  
Do remember that most verbs can be used both transitively as well as intransitively. For example,  
- The lawyer spoke the truth.  
- The teacher spoke softly.  

Sometime the subject and the object refer to the same person or thing. We then say that the verb has been used reflexively.  
- The girl cut **herself**.  

In such sentences, the object is sometimes omitted.  
- The tyre burst (itself).

**Verbs of Incomplete Predication**  
We know that intransitive verbs do not require objects.  

Look at these sentences.  
- The child **cries**.  
- Men **eat**.  
- Tigers **roar**.  

he highlighted verbs in these sentences do not require any words to make complete sense.  

An intransitive verb that makes complete sense on its own is called **a verb of complete predication**.  

Sometimes, an intransitive verb requires another word or words to make complete sense and to complete the predicate.  

An intransitive verb that requires a word or phrase to complete its meaning is called a verb of incomplete predication. The word or words required to make the sense complete is called **the complement of the verb**.  

For example,  
- The boy appears **sad**.  
- She is **talented**.  
- She seems **happy**.  
- The child continued **laughing**.  

In the above examples, the complements tell us something about the subject. So, these are called **subjective complements**.  

We know that transitive verbs have objects. Sometimes, just an object is not good enough. Something more is required to complete the predicate.  

Look at this sentence.  
- The members elected Sam captain.  
Here the word captain refers to Sam, the object of the verb. So captain is the objective complement.
