# Adjectives  

Words that are used to describe a noun or a pronoun are called **adjectives**. Adjectives help us to know more about nouns and pronouns by expressing their qualities.  

**Kinds of Adjectives**  
There are six kinds of adjectives. They are—  
- adjectives of quality  
- adjectives of quantity  
- adjectives of number  
- demonstrative adjectives  
- interrogative adjectives  
- emphasising adjectives  

Read these sentences.  
- He is a **brave** soldier.  
- Mana is a **kind** girl.  

The highlighted adjectives tell us **what kind** of noun is being spoken about.  

**Adjectives of quality** express the kind or quality of a person or a thing.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- I need some money to buy stationery.  
- There is not much ink in the bottle.  

The highlighted adjectives tell us what kind **how much** of a thing is meant.  

**Adjectives of quantity** tell us how much of a thing is meant.  

Look at these sentences.  
- This box contains ten apples.  
- Today is the last day of the festival.  
The highlighted adjectives answer the question **how many** or in **what order**.  

**Adjectives of number** tell us how many people or things are meant or in what order a person or a thing stands.  

Adjectives of number are of three kinds.  
- **Definite numeral adjectives** tell us the exact number of people or things. They are of two kinds—  
    - **cardinals** state the number of people or things. For example, forty thieves, twenty-five years.
    - **ordinals** give the order of people or things in a series. For example, first man on the moon, last day of the week.
- **Indefinite numeral adjectives** do not indicate the exact number. For example, few children, many people.  
- **Distributive numeral adjectives** refer to each one of a number.  
    - **Every** person must attend the meeting.
    - **Each** girl should participate in the competition.  

**NOTE**:  
Some adjectives can be classified as adjectives of number as well as adjectives of quantity depending on their usage in a sentence. Look at the examples given below.  

| Adjective of quantity       | Adjective of number                    |  
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |  
| He has lost all his money.  | All participants must submit a letter. |  
| He did not drink any water. | Are there any charts in the book?      |  

Look at these sentences.  
- This book is expensive.  
- These apples are from Himachal Pradesh.  

**Demonstrative adjectives** point out which person or thing is meant.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- Which pen do you want to buy?  
- Whose house is that?  
The highlighted adjectives are used to ask questions about the nouns that follow them.  

Words like **what, which** and **whose** when used with nouns to ask questions are called **interrogative adjectives**.  

Take a look at these sentences.  
- The campaign was a complete success.  
- This is the very dress that I wanted to buy.  
The highlighted adjectives are used to lay emphasis on the nouns that follow.  

The adjectives that we use to emphasise what we want to say are called **emphasising adjectives**.  

**NOTE**:  
- **Some** and **any** are used in affirmative and negative sentences, respectively. For example,  
    - I have bought **some** pens.
    - I have not purchased **any** pens.
- **Little** is used with uncountable nouns and **few** with countable nouns. For example,  
    - There is **little** hope of recovery.
    - There were a **few** chairs scattered around the room.
- **Few** means not many, while **a few** means some. For example,  
    - Sometimes, **a few** birds perch on this tree.
    - **Few** students were at school today.
- **Older** and **oldest** indicate age and are used to compare people or things. **Elder** and **eldest** are used with reference to family members. For example,  
    - My **eldest** brother will arrive today.
    - Jagriti is **older** than Mahesh.
    - This is the **oldest** house in the colony.
- **Later** and **latest** denote time and **latter** and **last** denote position.  
    - I am rather busy now and will see you **later**.
    - Which is the **latest** magazine?
    - Go and sit on the **last** bench.
    - Jaya and Rajeev are both good swimmers, but the **latter** is a more sporting loser.
- **Nearest** denotes distance and **next** denotes position.  
    - How far is the **nearest** bus stop?
    - Sita's house is **next** to mine.

**Participal Adjectives**  

Look at these sentences.  
- We stood in front of a **running** stream.  
- We heard the **interesting** news.  
The highlighted words are **present participles (verb + -ing form)**.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- People say that this is a haunted house.  
- The burnt cottage was razed to the ground.  
The highlighted words are **past participles (verb + -ed/-t/-n form)**.  

You will notice that in these sentences, the present participles and the past participles have been used as adjectives.  

Present participles and past participles, when used as adjectives, are called **participial adjectives**.

**Degrees of Comparison**  

You have learnt that adjectives have three degrees of comparison—positive, comparative and superlative. Let us revise what we know.  

Read these sentences.  
- This is a good violin.  
- My violin is better than the one you bought.  
- Bobby's violin is the best of all our violins.  

The adjective **good** is used when we talk about one person or thing. It is said to be in the **positive degree**.  

The **positive degree** is used when no comparison is made.  

The adjective **better** is used to compare two things. It conveys a higher degree of quality than that of the positive. The adjective **better** is said to be in the **comparative degree**.  

The **comparative degree** is used to compare two people or things.  

The adjective **best** is used to compare more than two things. The adjective best is said to be in the **superlative degree**.  

The **superlative degree** can be used to compare more than two things. It denotes the highest degree of quality.  

**NOTE**:  
When we use the comparative degree, we always use the word than to compare and contrast two things.  
The superlative degree, on the other hand, is always preceded by the word the.

**Interchange of Degrees of Comparison**  

Degrees of comparison of adjectives in sentences can be changed without any change in the meaning of the sentence. For example,  

- **No other** boy in the class is **as intelligent as** Gitesh. (**positive**)  
- Gitesh is **more intelligent than** any other boy in the class. (**comparative**)  
- Gitesh is the most intelligent boy in the class. (**superlative**)
