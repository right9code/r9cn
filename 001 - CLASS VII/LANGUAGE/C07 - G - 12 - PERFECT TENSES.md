# Perfect Tenses  

The perfect tenses show that an action has already been completed. The word perfect means completed.  

The **present perfect tense** tells us that an action is complete with respect to the present. For example,  
- I **have seen** this movie twice this week. (And the week isn't over yet.)  

The **past perfect tense** tells us that an action took place before another action in the past. For example,  
- Mala **had left** before I reached the office.  

The **future perfect tense** talks about an action that will occur in the future before some other action. For example,  
- I **will have arrived** before you come home.  

**Present Perfect Tense**  
The present perfect is used—  
- to indicate that an action has been completed just now or a little while ago. For example,  
    - He **has just finished** correcting answer scripts.
    - The sun **has risen** a while ago.
- to express an action whose time is not given and is not definite. For example,  
    - We **have known** her to be very helpful.
- to describe a past event when we think more about its effect on the present. For example,  
    - I **have broken** my glasses. (So, I cannot see properly.)
- to denote an action that started some time ago and is continuing till the time of speaking. For example,  
    - We **have known** the family for many years.
    - Manju **has been** ill since Sunday.  

**NOTE**:  
Both the simple past tense and the present perfect tense express an action completed in the past. But the simple past tense expresses an action completed in the past, but not linked to the present. The present perfect tense, however, denotes an action that is completed but connected to the present.  
The present perfect tense is never used with adverbs or phrases of past time like **yesterday**, **last month**, etc.  
We do not say, He has **gone** to the museum yesterday.  
We say, He **went** to the museum yesterday.  

**Past Perfect Tense**  
We use the past perfect tense to denote—  
- an action completed before a given point of time. This moment or point of time may be indicated or may be understood.  
    - When I reached the station, the train **had left**.
    - I **had completed** my training by noon.  

**NOTE**:  
If two actions occurred in the past, with one action taking place earlier than the other, the past perfect is used for the earlier action and the simple past for the later action. Thus, we say,  
- The exam had started when I **reached** school.  

The past perfect tense is used with such verbs as hope, expect, think to indicate that a past hope or desire remained unfulfilled.  
- I had believed that I would win the tournament but I did not.  

**Future Perfect Tense**  
The future perfect tense is used—  
- to refer to the completion of an action by a certain time in the future. The tense is normally used with a time expression beginning with by, such as, by that time, by then or by next week.  
    - The Minister will have announced his decision by next week.
    - They will have completed the assignment by next summer.
    - The jury will have given its verdict by noon tomorrow.
    - By next week, we shall have reached an agreement regarding the factory.
