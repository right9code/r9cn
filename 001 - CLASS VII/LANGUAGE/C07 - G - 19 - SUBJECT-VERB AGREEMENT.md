# Subject-Verb Agreement  

As a rule, the verb in a sentence always agrees with the subject in number and person. If the subject is singular, the verb is singular. If the subject is plural, the verb is also plural. For example,  
- **The baby** cries.  
- **Babies** cry.  

Sometimes, it is difficult to tell whether the subject is singular or plural. Here are a few hints that will help you find out if the subject is singular or plural.  

- Two or more singular subjects connected by **and** usually take a verb in the plural. For example,  
    - **Rahul and Ravi** ***are*** here.
- If two singular nouns refer to the same person or thing, the verb must be singular. For example,  
    - **My friend and neighbour** ***is*** here.
- If two subjects together express one idea, the verb may be in the singular. For example,  
    - **The long and short of the matter** ***is*** that she has decided to return.
- If the subject is preceded by **each** or **every**, the verb is usually singular. For example,  
    - **Every man, woman and child** ***was*** present at the gathering.
      - **Each child** ***wants*** an ice cream.
- Two or more singular subjects connected by **or, nor, neither...nor, either...or** take a verb in the singular. For example,  
    - **Neither Rita nor I** ***was*** prepared.
- When the subjects joined by **or, nor** take different persons, the verb agrees in person with the one nearest to it. For example,  
    - Either he or **I** ***am*** to blame.
    - Neither Amit nor **his friends** ***have*** seen that movie.
- When the subjects joined by **or, nor** are of different numbers, the verb must be plural, and the plural subject must be placed next to the verb. For example,  
    - Neither Susan nor **her parents** ***were*** ready.
- When subjects differing in number or person or both are connected by **and**, the verb must always be in the plural. For example,  
    - **She and I** ***are*** leaving the job.
- A collective noun takes a singular verb when the collection is thought of as a whole. A plural verb is used when the collection is thought of separate individuals. For example,  
    - **A number of members of the team** ***were*** absent.
    - **The jury** ***has*** agreed to our proposal.
- Nouns which are plural in form but singular in meaning, take a singular verb. For example,  
    - **The news** ***is*** true.
- Words joined to a singular subject by **along with, together with, as well as** take a singular verb.  
    - **The general**, along with his regiment, ***has*** arrived.
- Class names such as **furniture, stationery**, take a singular verb.  
    - **The furniture** ***is*** of good quality.
- Words in which two components are always thought of together, as in, **scissors, spectacles, shoes, trousers**, take a singular verb.  
    - **The pair of trousers** ***is*** his.
- However, when we omit the words **a pair of**, such words then take a plural verb.  
    - **My shoes** ***are*** wet.
- If two or more singular subjects are preceded by **either, either of, neither of, each, each of, every one of, many a, none, none of, nobody** or **somebody**, the verb is in the singular.  
    - **Every one of us** ***is*** equal in the eyes of the Almighty.
      - **Somebody** ***has*** eaten the apple.
      - **Each of the girls** ***has*** performed well.
- When a plural number applies to distances, heights, weights, it is taken as a whole and is therefore treated as a singular.  
    - **Ten million rupees** ***is*** a lot of money.
    - **Ten kilos of apples** ***costs*** three hundred rupees.
- The quantifier **many** refers to numbers and is plural; **much** refers to amounts and is thus singular.  
    - **Many of the peaches** ***were*** ripe.
    - **Much of the rice** ***was*** saved from pests.
- Fractions take a plural verb if the reference is to number, but they take a singular verb if the reference is to amount or quantity.  
    - **Some** of the girls ***are*** sick.
    - **Some** of the milk ***has*** curdled.
