# Kinds of Clauses  

You have learnt that a **clause** is a group of words that contains a subject and a finite verb. For example:  
- because it is quite dark  
- but he is lazy  
- It is dark.  
- He is lazy.  

Now, take a look at these sentences.  
- I will leave now. It is getting late.  
    - I will leave now **because** it is getting late.
With the help of a conjunction, we have joined two sentences into one.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- **I will leave now** *because it is getting late*.  
- **He is a good boy** *but he is lazy*.  

The underlined parts of these sentences can stand on their own as independent sentences. The highlighted parts, however, cannot stand on their own. They have to depend on the rest of the sentence to convey the correct meaning.  

A clause that can stand on its own is called a **main**, or **principal, clause**.  

A clause that cannot stand on its own and needs a main clause to complete its meaning is called a **subordinate**, or **dependent clause**.

There are three kinds of subordinate clauses.  

**Noun Clauses**  

Read these sentences and observe the function of the highlighted group of words in each case.  
- **When he will arrive** is not clear. **(subject)**  
- The teacher does not know **why he is absent today**. **(object)**  
In these sentences, the highlighted groups of words are clauses that act exactly like nouns.  

A clause that functions like a noun in a sentence and often acts as the subject or the object of a verb is called a **noun clause**.

**Adjective Clauses**  
Now, read these sentences and observe the function of the highlighted clause in each.  
- He is the naughty boy **who broke the window**.  
- The cat played with the red ball **which was on the floor**.  
- The apartment **that he purchased** was very spacious.  

We notice that the clauses act as adjectives in these sentences. They tell us more about the **naughty boy**, the **red ball** and the **apartment**. The clauses are introduced by relative pronouns like **who**, **which** and **that**.  

A clause that functions like an adjective in a sentence is called an **adjective clause**.

**Adverb Clauses**  
Observe how the highlighted clauses modify the verbs in these sentences.  
- Work hard **so that you may fulfil your dreams**. **(purpose)**  
- The thief fled **when he saw a group of men approaching**. **(time)**  
- You can stay **where you like**. **(place)**  
- I will help him **because he needs my help**. **(reason)**  
- It was so cold **that all the schools had to be shut down**. **(result)**  
- **If I like the flat**, I shall buy it. **(condition)**

Clauses that function like adverbs and modify verbs, adjectives and other adverbs, are called **adverb clauses**.
