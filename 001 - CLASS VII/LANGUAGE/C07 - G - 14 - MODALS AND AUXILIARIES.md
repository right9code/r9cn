# Modals and Auxiliaries  

An auxiliary is a helping verb. In a sentence, an auxiliary is always used with another verb. There are two types of auxiliary verbs.  

- **primary auxiliaries:** The words be, have and do are called primary auxiliaries. The forms of be are **is, am, are, was and were**. The forms of have are **have, has and had**. The forms of do are **do, does and did**.  
- **modal auxiliaries:** The words **can, could, may, might, shall, should, must, will, would, used, need, dare, ought** express attitudes like permission, possibility, etc. Modal auxiliaries are also called modals. They do not change their forms.  

**Primary Auxiliaries**  

**Be**  
- It is used in the formation of continuous tenses. For example,  
    - Mother **is knitting** a sweater.
- It is used to show that the action is more important than the doer. For example,  
    - The cupboard **was** opened by Kirti.
- When followed by an infinitive, it is used to indicate a plan or an arrangement. For example,  
    - We **are** to finish the work in a month.
- It is also used to denote a command. For example,  
    - You **are** to pick it up right now.  

**Have**  
- This is used to form the perfect tenses. For example,  
    - The artists **have painted** beautifully.
- It is used with the infinitive to indicate some kind of obligation. For example,  
    - They **have** to return immediately.  

**Do**  
- It is used to form negative statements and interrogative sentences. For example,  
  - He **doesn't** agree with me.
  - **Did** you manage to finish the assignment?
- In imperative sentences, it is used to make the statement more forceful. For example,  
  - **Do** be quiet.  

**Modal Auxiliaries**  

**Can and Could**  
- **Can** usually suggests potential or ability. For example,  
    - Ram **can** take you to the airport.
    - **Can** you lift this?
- **Can** is also used to indicate permission. For example,  
    - You **can** join from tomorrow.
- **Could** is used to ask a polite question. For example,  
    - **Could** you help me solve the sum?
- **Could** is used as the past tense of can in indirect speech. For example,  
    - Toto said, 'I **can** help you.'
    - Toto said that he **could** help me.
- **Could** is used to indicate ability in the past. For example,  
    - He **could** sing well once upon a time.  

**May and Might**  
- **May** is usually used to request permission formally. For example,  
    - **May** I use your eraser?
- **May** is also used to express a wish. For example,  
    - **May** you always enjoy good luck!
- **May** is used to indicate possibility. For example,  
    - There **may** be some rain this evening.
- **May** suggests a weak possibility and **might** suggests a very weak possibility in the future. For example,  
    - The match schedule **may** be announced next Monday.
    - We **might** return sooner than expected.
    - She **might** arrive tomorrow.  

**Shall, Should, Will and Would**  
- **Shall** is usually used in the first person and will in the second and third person. For example,  
    - I **shall** be pleased to visit you.
    - You **will** agree with me.
- **Shall** is used in the second and third person to express a command, a promise or even a threat. For example,  
    - He **shall** not work here again. (threat)
    - You **shall** certainly be rewarded. (promise)
- **Shall** is used in interrogative sentences with the first person to indicate an offer or a suggestion. For example,  
    - **Shall** I carry these books for you?
- **Will** is used to express a decision or a choice. For example,  
    - I **will** do the cooking today.
- **Will** is used to indicate a habit. For example,  
    - He **will** go swimming as usual.
- **Will** is used in interrogative sentences to suggest an invitation. For example,  
    - **Will** you go on a holiday with us?
- In clauses starting with *if*, **should** is used to express a supposition that may not be true. For example,  
    - If it **should** rain, the match will be called off.  

**Must and Ought**  
- **Must** expresses necessity or obligation. For example,  
    - We **must** remember this.
- **Must** can express fixed determination. For example,  
    - I **must** have an opportunity to do what I wish.
- **Must** and **ought** also express certainty or strong possibility. For example,  
    - He **must** have forgotten about the invitation.
    - We **ought** to strike this deal.
- **Ought** expresses moral obligation or desirability. For example,  
    - We **ought** to try hard.  

**Used**  
- It is used to indicate a discontinued habit. For example,  
  - She **used** to learn ballet.  

**Need**  
- It denotes necessity or obligation. It is commonly used in negatives and interrogatives. For example,  
    - He **need** not give up tennis.
    - **Need** I help him?
- **Need** can also be used to denote requirement or desire. For example,  
    - I do not **need** your help.  

**Dare**  
**Dare** as modal auxiliary means 'be bold enough to'.  
- It is commonly used in interrogative and negative sentences.  
    - How **dare** she say this?
- **Dare** can also be used to indicate a challenge. For example,  
    - I **dare** you to eat all the food on the table.
