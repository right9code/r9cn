# Adverbs  

We know that an **adverb** is a word that qualifies, or modifies, a verb, an adjective or another adverb. For example,  
- He ran **fast**. (modifies the verb, **ran**)  
- The apple was **very** sweet. (modifies the adjective, **sweet**)  
- He walked **rather** slowly. (modifies the adverb, **slowly**)  

**Kinds of Adverb**  
**Adverbs of manner** tell us how an action occurred. For example,  
- He laughed **loudly**.  

**Adverbs of place** tell us where an action occurred. For example,  
- I lived **here**.  

**Adverbs of time** tell us when an action occurred. For example,  
- I have done this **before**.  

**Adverbs of frequency** tell us how often an action occurs. For example,  
- I fell down **twice**.  

**Adverbs of degree** tell us the degree or extent of an action or a quality. For example,  
- I was very **sick**.  

**Adverbs of reason** state a reason; they answer the question why? For example,  
- He received a call from his father. Therefore, he **left**.  

Adverbs that are used in asking questions are called **interrogative adverbs**. For example,  
- Where is Mira? (interrogative adverb of place)  
- When did the train leave? (interrogative adverb of time)  
- Why did you cry? (interrogative adverb of reason)  
- How did you manage this? (interrogative adverb of manner)

**Formation of Adverbs**  
Adverbs are formed from adjectives in the following ways:  

- Most adjectives add -ly to form adverbs. For example, **rude—rudely, quick—quickly**.  
- In an adjective ending in -y preceded by a consonant, the -y is changed into -i and -ly is added. For example, **happy—happily, angry—angrily**.  
- When the adjective ends in -e, the -e is dropped and -ly is added. For example, **true—truly, double—doubly**.

**Comparison of Adverbs**  
Adverbs, like adjectives, can be used to make comparisons. The degrees of comparison are positive, comparative and superlative. For example,  
- Rahim writes **neatly**. **(positive)**  
- His brother writes **more neatly** than him. **(comparative)**  
- Their sister writes the **most neatly**. **(superlative)**  
- We form the comparative and the superlative forms by adding -er and -est, respectively, to the positive degree of most adverbs. For example, fast—faster—fastest, hard—harder—hardest.  
- We also form the comparatives and the superlatives by adding more and most, respectively, to the positive degree of many adverbs. For example, happily—more happily—most happily.  
- Some adverbs form the comparatives and the superlatives in an irregular way. For example, little—less—least, much—more—most.

**Position of Adverbs**  
- **Adverbs of manner, time** and **place** are placed after transitive verbs or after the object if the verb is transitive. For example,  
    - Arun sang **loudly**. **(adverb of manner)**
    - Cross the road **carefully**. **(adverb of manner)**
    - Put the book **there**. **(adverb of place)**
    - They started **early**. **(adverb of time)**
    - I wrote a poem **yesterday**. **(adverb of time)**  

- **Adverbs of frequency** and **degree** are placed between the subject and the verb if the sentence contains only one verb. For example,  
    - He **always** meets people in the afternoon. **(adverb of frequency)**  

- Adverbs are also placed after the first auxiliary or helping verb if the verb consists of more than one word. For example,  
    - The guests **have** just **arrived**.  

- Adverbs are placed after the forms of the verb, **be (am, is, was, were)**. For example,  
    - He is seldom late.  

 - When an adverb modifies an adjective or another adverb, it usually comes before the word it modifies. For example,
    - It is a **rather** sultry day.  

**NOTE**:  
The position of the adverb needs careful handling, as the position alters the implication of the sentence. For example,  
- David only reached Pune yesterday.  
- Only David reached Pune yesterday.

Let us look at the use of some common adverbs that are often confused.  

- The adverbs **very** and **too** are used with an adjective or another adverb.  
    - He did the work **very** nicely.
    - She is **too** lazy to finish the work.  

**NOTE**:  
Both the adverbs, very and too, indicate an excess, but they are usually not interchangeable.  
- It is too cold to go outside. (correct)  
- It is very cold to go outside. (incorrect)  
- It is very cold today. (correct)  
- It is too cold today. (incorrect)  
- The adverbs **fairly** and **rather** both mean 'moderately'. But **fairly** is chiefly used with favourable adjectives and adverbs, while **rather** is used before unfavourable ones.  
    - The weather is **fairly** pleasant.
    - I received some **rather** bad news.  

- We use **much** to qualify an adjective or an adverb in the comparative degree while **very** is used along with the positive.  
    - He is much better now.
    - He walked very slowly.

Otherwise, we **never** (Adverb of Frequency) worried, we just relaxed and drove **around** (Adverb of Place) in the rain.
