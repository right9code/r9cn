# Modals  

Read this conversation. Observe the function of the highlighted words.  

**Rakhi**: May I come in, ma'am?  
**Teacher**: You must not be late for class.  
**Rakhi**: I'm sorry, ma'am. I had to meet the maths teacher, but I could not find her, so I got late. It will not happen again.  
**Teacher**: Very well. But you ought to excuse yourself first.  

The highlighted words above are verbs that are tell us more about the mood or the attitude of the speaker. You have learnt that such words are called **modals**.  

**Modals**, or **modal auxiliaries**, are verbs that are used to express the mood or attitude of the speaker.  

**NOTE**:  
You have learnt that auxiliaries, or helping verbs, are of two kinds—primary auxiliaries and modal auxiliaries. The primary auxiliaries are the verbs be, have and do when they are used with other verbs. Look at this table that lists the forms and functions of these verbs.  

| Forms                                    | Functions                                                                     | Examples                                                                               |     |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --- |
| be (present—is, am, are; past—was, were) | form continuous tenses, used in the passive and to denote a command or a plan | - I am writing a letter. - The door was opened by Shyam. - You are to leave right now. |     |
| have (present—has, have; past—had)       | form the perfect tenses                                                       | - I have completed my work. - Ma had made orange pudding.                              |     |
| do (present—does, do; past—did)          | form interrogatives, imperatives and negative sentences                       | - Do you know that girl? - Do be patient. - He doesn't agree.                          |     |  

The verbs be, have and do can also be used as principal, or main verbs. For example,  
- I am a student. **(reveals a state of being)**  
- I have a pet dog. **(indicates ownership)**  
- I do yoga every day. **(shows action)**  

The most commonly used modals are **can, could, may, might, shall, should, must, will, would, used, need, dare** and **ought**. These are used to express possibility, certainty, necessity, ability, expectation, permission, advice, wish or obligation. Let us now study the use of modal auxiliaries in detail.  

**NOTE**:  
Modals are always followed by a main verb. They do not change form with the person or the number of the subject.  

**Can is used—**  
- to talk about ability and possibility. For example,  
    - She can play tennis very well. **(ability)**
    - Loud noise can damage your ears. **(possibility)**
- to ask for and give permission in an informal manner. For example,  
    - Can I join the gym from tomorrow? **(seeking permission)**
    - You can meet me tomorrow. **(giving permission)**
**Could is used—**  
- as the past equivalent of can in indirect speech. For example,  
    - Mani said, 'I can help you.'
    - Mani said that she could help me.
- to make polite requests. For example,  
    - Could you help me carry these bags?
 - to indicate ability in the past. For example,
    - She could do handstands once upon a time.
**May is used—**  
- to ask for permission in a formal manner. For example,  
    - May I come in, sir?
-  to express a wish. For example,  
    - May you always enjoy good luck!
- to indicate a weak possibility. For example,  
    - It may rain this afternoon.
**Might is used—**  
- to suggest a very weak possibility in the future. For example,  
    - It might rain tonight.
    - Don't play with the knives. You might get hurt.
- as the past equivalent of may in indirect speech. For example,  
    - Jiya said, 'I may get the job.'
    - Jiya said that she might get the job.
**Will is used—**  
- to talk about future events that are planned or about which we are certain. For example,  
    - The train will leave at 10 a.m.
    - My mother will turn sixty this year.
- to show determination or willingness. For example,  
    - I will help you complete the assignment.
- to make polite requests. For example,  
    - Will you help me complete the assignment?
    - Will you turn down the music, please? I am trying to sleep.
**Shall is used—**  
- to make offers or suggestions. For example,  
    - Shall I close the door?
- to show certainty or promise. For example,  
    - I shall clean the kitchen tomorrow.
- in the second and third person to express a command, a promise or even a threat. For example,  
    - He shall not come here again. (threat)
    - You shall certainly receive a raise. (promise)  

**Would is used—**  
- to make polite requests. For example,  
    - Would you mind lending me your pen?
- to talk about past habits. For example,  
    - After dinner, we would sit and talk for hours.  

**NOTE**:  
Shall is usually used with the first person singular (I) or the first person plural (we).  
- to talk about imaginary situations. For example,  
- I would love to go to Morocco.  

**Should is used—**  
- to express advice, suggestion or obligation. For example,  
- You should tell the truth.  
- to express probability. For example,  
- You should find this grammar book helpful.  
**Must is used—**  
- to express necessity or obligation. For example,  
    - We must do this right away.
- to give advice to someone, especially when we want to emphasis that it is for their own good. For example,  
    - You must stop lying.
**Ought to is used—**  
- to express duty or moral obligation. For example,  
    - We ought to obey our parents. (duty)
    - We ought to protect our national monuments. (moral obligation)
- to express necessity. For example,  
    - We ought to buy a cupboard.
**Used is used—**  
- to indicate something that was a habit, but has been discontinued. For example,  
    - She used to learn yoga.
**Need is used—**  
- to denote necessity or obligation. For example,  
    - Jaya need not go to the post office.
    - Do I need to help him?  

**NOTE**:  
As an ordinary verb, need means want or require. For example,  
- I do not need your help.  

**Dare is used—**  
- in interrogative and negative sentences where the implication is to be **bold enough to**.  
    - How dare you say so?
- to imply a challenge. For example,  
    - I dare you to finish up the whole pizza.  

**NOTE**:  
Notice how the use of modals affects the meaning of a sentence.  
- You must do it. (means it is necessary that you do it)  
- You mustn't do it. (means it is necessary that you do not do it)  
- You needn't do it. (means it is not necessary for you to do it)  
- You ought to do it. (expresses moral obligation or desirability)
