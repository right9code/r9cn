# Kinds of Clauses  

You have learnt that a clause is a group of words that contains a subject and a finite verb.  

Some sentences are made up of one clause. Some sentences are made up of two or more clauses. Let us first understand the differences between a phrase, a clause and a sentence.  

| Phrase                                     | Clause                                                                 | Sentence                                   |  
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |  
| A phrase is a meaningful group of words.   | A clause is a meaningful group of words.                               | A sentence is a meaningful group of words. |  
| It does not make complete sense by itself. | It may or may not be dependent on something else for complete meaning. | It expresses a complete thought.           |  
| It has no subject or predicate.            | It has a subject and a predicate.                                      | It has a subject and a predicate.          |  
| It has no finite verb.                     | It has a finite verb.                                                  | It has a finite verb.                      |  

Now, read this sentence.  
- *The girl* **who came yesterday** was Manish's sister.  

The highlighted part of this sentence can stand on its own. However, the underlined part of this sentence cannot stand on its own. It has to depend on the rest of the sentence to convey the correct meaning.  

A clause that can stand on its own is called the main clause, or the *principal clause*.  

A clause that cannot stand on its own and depends on the main clause to complete its meaning is called the subordinate clause, or the *dependent clause*.  

Now, read this sentence.  
- Mahima is writing a letter.  

This sentence has a subject and a predicate **and** can stand on its own; so we can say that this sentence is a main clause.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- The girl tried. The girl succeeded.  
- The girl tried **and** succeeded.  

By using the coordinating conjunction and, we joined two sentences of equal grammatical importance.  
- Clauses that are joined by coordinating conjunctions are called **coordinate clauses**, or **coordinate main clauses**.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- He has a car. He prefers to travel by bus.  
  He has a car **but** prefers to travel by bus.
By using the coordinating conjunction but, we joined two sentences showing contrasting ideas.

You have learnt that a subordinate clause cannot stand on its own. It usually depends on the main clause to complete its  

meaning. There are three types of subordinate clauses. Let us learn more about them.  

**Adjective Clauses**  

Read these sentences.  
- This is the book **that I am reading**.  
- There is the woman **who took my interview**.  

The highlighted clauses in these sentences are subordinate clauses that do the work of **an adjective**—they modify the nouns book and woman.  

The nouns **book** and **woman** are called **antecedents** to the relative pronouns that and who, respectively.  

A subordinate clause that does the work of an adjective is called **an adjective clause**.  

An adjective clause should be placed as close as possible to the noun or pronoun it modifies, ideally right next to it.  

An adjective clause about persons begins with the relative pronouns **who, whom** or **whose**.  
These pronouns refer to the person in the main clause. For example,  
- I have a penfriend **who** lives in New York.  
- The artist **whom** I adored is no more.  

**NOTE**:  
An adjective clause is also known as an adjectival clause, or relative clause.  

An adjective clause about animals or things begins with which, that or those. These refer to the animals or things in the main clause. For example,  
- This is the watch **that** my uncle gave me.  
- This is the road **which** leads to the stadium.  

The words **when, where** and **why** can also be used like relative pronouns to begin an adjective clause referring to a place, a time or a reason in the main clause. For example,  
- Do you know the time **when** the movie begins?  
- I went to the town **where** I was born.  
- No one knows the reason **why** we lost.

**Noun Clauses**  
A **noun clause** is a subordinate clause that does the work of a noun in a sentence.  

Read these sentences.  
- The teacher said that the earth is round.  
- Richa asked me what I wanted.  
- I am thinking whether I should go to the party or not.  

In these sentences, the clauses act as the object or the complement of the verb. Hence, they function like nouns.  

A noun clause can function as the subject of a verb, the object of a verb, the object of a preposition or the subjective complement. For example,  

- **What the child said** was true. **(subject)**  
- I wish to know **how this works.** **(object)**  
- My health depends on **how well I take care of myself**. **(object of a preposition)**  
- The climber's greatest fear was **that he might not reach the peak**. **(subjective complement)**  

We can combine two independent sentences by changing one to a noun clause by using—  
- pronouns like **who, what, which, whom** and **whose**. For example,  
    - I know **who** Karan is.
- conjunctions like **that, if** or **whether**. For example,  
    - I wonder **if** Kavita knows how to cook.
- adverbs like **when, where, why** and **how**. For example,  
    - I wonder **where** Mohan is.

**Adverb Clauses**  
An **adverb clause** is a subordinate clause that does the work of an adverb in a sentence. It modifies the meaning of a verb, an adjective or another adverb in the main clause.  

Adverb clauses show relationships such as time, cause and effect, contrast and condition. Therefore, adverb clauses are classified into the following types.  

**Adverb Clauses**  

An **adverb clause** is a subordinate clause that does the work of an adverb in a sentence. It modifies the meaning of a verb, an adjective, or another adverb in the main clause.  

Adverb clauses show relationships such as **time**, **cause and effect**, **contrast**, and **condition**. Therefore, adverb clauses are classified into the following types.  

1. **Adverb clauses of time** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **while, after, when, before, since, whenever, till, as long as, as soon as**.  
    - For example: *We must reach **before** the bus leaves.*  

2. **Adverb clauses of place** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **where, wherein, wherever**.  
    - For example: *Keep the watch **where** it will be safe.*  

3. **Adverb clauses of purpose** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **that, so that, in order that, lest**.  
    - For example: *We eat so **that** we may grow strong.*  

4. **Adverb clauses of reason** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **since, because, as**.  
    - For example: *I must leave now **because** I am feeling unwell.*  

5. **Adverb clauses of condition** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **if, whether, unless, in case, so long as, provided that**.  
    - For example: ***If** you like it, I shall get it for you.*  

6. **Adverb clauses of result** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **that**, which may be preceded by **so** or **such**.  
    - For example: *It was so hot **that** the plant died.*  

7. **Adverb clauses of comparison** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **than** and **as...as**.  
    - For example: *The boy is taller **than** his sister.*  

8. **Adverb clauses of concession** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **though, although, as, notwithstanding that, even if, however**.  
    - For example: ***Though** I am not wealthy, I will still be a successful businessman.*  

9. **Adverb clauses of manner** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **like, as, as if, as though, just as**.  
    - For example: *She was walking **as if** she was in a hurry.*  

10. **Adverb clauses of contrast** are introduced by the subordinating conjunctions **while** and **whereas**.  
    - For example: *I was reading **while** my brother was watching television.*
