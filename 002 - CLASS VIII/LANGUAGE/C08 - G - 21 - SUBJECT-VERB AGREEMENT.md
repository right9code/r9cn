# Subject-Verb Agreement  

You have learnt that a verb always agrees with its subject in number and in person. For example,  
- **He** *is* ill.  
- **I** *am* an architect.  
- **Elephants** *love* bananas.  
- **My mother** *exercises* every day.  
- **They** *were going* to the museum.  

Let us revise the rules that help us determine if the subject is singular or plural.  
- If two or more singular nouns or pronouns are joined by the conjunction and, we use a plural verb. For example,  
    - **Mita and Rita** *are* students of this school.
    - **The teacher and the student** *were* present.
- When two nouns suggest the same idea or refer to the same person or thing, we use a singular verb. For example,  
    - **The rise and fall of the Mughal Empire** *is* the subject of his study.
    - **Slow and steady** *wins* the race.
- If two subjects together express one idea, the verb may be in the singular. For example,  
    - **The long and short of the matter** *is* that she has decided to return to England.
- If the singular subjects are preceded by **each** or **every**, the verb is usually singular. For example,  
    - **Every man, woman and child** *was* present at the gathering.
- A collective noun takes a singular verb when the group is considered as a unit. For example,  
    - **The committee** *has* accepted your resignation.
- However, if we are referring to the members of the unit individually, a plural verb is used. For example,  
    - **The committee members** *are* divided in their opinion.
- Some nouns are plural in form, but singular in meaning. Such nouns take a singular verb. For example,  
    - **Physics** *is* my favourite subject.
    - **The news** *was* scary.
- Some nouns are singular in form, but plural in meaning and thus take a plural verb. For example,  
    - **The police** *have* nabbed the culprit.
- The words **each**, **everyone**, **neither...nor**, **either...or** take a singular verb. For example,  
    - **Everyone** *was* welcome.
    - Neither he nor **I** *was* prepared.
- When two subjects joined by **either...or** and **neither...nor** take different persons, the verb agrees in person with the one nearest to it. For example,  
    - Either you or **she** *is* telling the truth.
- If the subjects joined by **either...or** and **neither...nor** are of different numbers, the verb agrees in number with the subject that is closest to it. For example,  
    - The boy or **his brothers** *have* made the mistake.
    - Neither the team members nor **the captain** *has* reached the station.
- When two subjects are joined by **as well as**, **with**, **along with**, etc., the verb agrees with the first subject/noun, that is, if the first noun is in the singular, the verb must be singular, even if the second noun is plural. For example,  
    - **Rita**, along with her friends, *has* won the prize.
- A relative pronoun always agrees in number and person with its antecedent. For example,  
    - He is one of those **men** who *know* a lot about this matter.
- **Many** refers to number, and is plural. **Much** refers to amount, and is singular. For example,  
    - **Many** of the bananas *were* ripe.
    - **Much** of the milk *was* wasted.
- When a plural number applies to distances, weights, etc., it is taken as a whole, and therefore treated as singular. For example,  
    - **Two kilos of meat** *costs* five hundred rupees.
    - **Three hundred miles** *is* not a short distance.
- Uncountable nouns such as **clothing, news, stationery, cutlery** are treated as singular and therefore take a singular verb. For example,  
    - **The furniture** *is* expensive.
- Both always takes a plural verb. For example,  
    - **Both the girls** *were* scolded.
- When the subject has the phrase **one of the**, followed by a plural noun, the verb is singular. The real subject is **one**. For example,  
    - **One** of the boys in the class *was* praised by the principal.
- When an **of** phrase follows a percentage, fraction or an amount, the verb agrees with the noun/pronoun closest to it. For example,  
    - One-third of the **population** *is* poor.
    - One-third of the **villagers** *have* brick houses here.
- With quantifiers like all, some and few, the verb agrees with the noun/pronoun closest to it. For example,  
    - Some of the **banana cake** *is* in the tin.
    - Some of the **muffins** *were* very hard.  

**NOTE**:  
Often a verb is made to agree with a noun near it instead of its proper subject. This is incorrect and should be avoided.  
- The behaviour of the students were excellent. (incorrect)  
- The **behaviour** of the students was excellent. (correct)  
- The cost of all these goods have risen. (incorrect)  
- The **cost** of all these goods has risen. (correct)
