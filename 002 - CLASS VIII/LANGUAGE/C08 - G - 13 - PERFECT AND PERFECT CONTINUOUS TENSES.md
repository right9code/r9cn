# Perfect and Perfect Continuous Tenses  

You have learnt that the perfect tenses show that an action has already been completed. The word **perfect** implies **completed**. Let us revise what you have learnt about the perfect tenses.  

**Present Perfect Tense**  
Both the simple past tense and the present perfect tense express an action completed in the past. The simple past tense expresses an action completed in the past but not linked to the present. The present perfect tense, however, denotes an action that is completed but connected to the present. The present perfect tense is used—  

- to indicate an action that has been completed just now or a little while ago. For example,  
    - They **have bought** a car.
    - I **have called** for a cab.
- to express an action whose time is not given and is not definite. For example,  
    - He **has lived** in Bangkok.
- to describe a past event which has its effect on the present. For example,  
    - I **have lost** the keys to the house. (So I cannot open the door.)
- to talk about a continuing state, not action. For example,  
    - Girish **has been** ill since Monday.
    - Mini **has broken** her leg.
    - We **have known** the Guptas for twenty years.  

**NOTE**:  
We use since to talk about a point of time in the past like Tuesday, 9 o'clock. We use for to talk about a period or duration of time like two weeks, five years.  

**Past Perfect Tense**  
The past perfect tense is used to denote an action that took place before another action. For example,  
- When I reached the bus stop, the bus **had left**.  

The tenses of the verbs in this sentence show which action happened first. The past perfect **(had left)** is used for the earlier action and the simple past (**reached**) for the later action.  

The past perfect tense is also used to indicate a past hope or desire that has remained unfulfilled. We use verbs like hope, expect, think in such cases. For example,  
- I **had expected** to win the tournament.  

**Future Perfect Tense**  
The future perfect tense is used to refer to the completion of an action by a certain time in the future. The tense is normally used with expressions like **by that time, by then, by next week**. For example,  
- **I will have completed** the assignment by next week.  
- The coach **will have arrived** by 11:00 a.m.

**Present Perfect Continuous Tense**  

The present perfect continuous is used—  
- to denote an action that began at some time in the past and is still continuing. For example,  
    - I **have been swimming** for two hours.
    - Kate **has been working** since six in the morning.
- to denote repeated actions. For example,  
    - I **have been getting** this journal every week for a year.  

**NOTE**:  
There is sometimes very little difference between the meaning of the present perfect and the meaning of the present perfect continuous tense.  
- I have been working at this site for three years.  
- I have worked here for three years.  

**Past Perfect Continuous Tense**  
The past perfect continuous is used—  
- to denote that an action that began before a particular moment in the past and was still taking place, or had just finished, at that moment when another action took place. For example,  
    - When I visited Gwalior, he **had been teaching** there for a year.
    - I **had been driving** for an hour when I heard a sound in the gear box.
- to talk about repeated actions in the past. For example,  
    - She **had been trying** to fix this all day.  

**Future Perfect Continuous Tense**  
The future perfect continuous is used—  
- to indicate an action has been in progress over a period of time and will end in the future. For example,  
    - By the end of the year, I **shall have been living** in Delhi for seven years.
    - When their mother returns home at five o'clock, Rita **will have been watching** television for four hours.
