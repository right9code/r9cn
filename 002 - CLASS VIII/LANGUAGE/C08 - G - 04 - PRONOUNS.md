# Pronouns  

A **pronoun** is a word that is used instead of a noun. Look at these sentences.  
- Jaideep is a very hardworking boy. He is good at studies as well as in sports. His brothers admire him. Both of them would like to follow in his footsteps.  
Here, the words he and **him** take the place of Jaideep, while them is used instead of **his brothers**.  

The noun which is replaced by a pronoun is known as its **antecedent**.  

Pronouns are of different kinds. Let us revise what you have learnt.  

- personal  
- demonstrative  
- reflexive  
- distributive  
- indefinite  
- possessive  
- interrogative  
- emphasising  
- reciprocal  
- relative  

The pronouns **I, me, she, her, he, him, you, we, us, it, they** and **them** come in the place of people, animals or things. So they are called **personal pronouns**.  

**NOTE**:  
Pronouns agree with their antecedents in gender, number and person.  

For example,  
- These boys are lazy. Their teacher has given them a lot of homework, but **they** are not interested in completing **it**.  

In the above sentences, the pronoun it replaces the noun **homework** while these boys is the antecedent for the pronouns **they** and **them**.  

Note that we use **they** when the pronoun is the subject of the verb and them when it acts as the object.  

The pronouns **mine, ours, his, hers, its, theirs** and **yours** are used to show possession. So they are called **possessive pronouns**.  

For example,  
- Jayshree has a small zoo in her house. That cat is hers.  
- The decision is yours to make.

Pronouns that are used to point out the objects to which they refer are called **demonstrative pronouns**.  

**NOTE**:  
We use **this** and **these** to indicate objects that are nearby while **that** and **those** are used for objects that are far away. A less commonly used demonstrative pronoun is **yonder**.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- **Whom** did you call up?  
- **Which** of these boxes is yours?  
- **What** is the name of that girl?  

Pronouns that help to ask questions are called **interrogative pronouns**.  

**NOTE**:  
When framing questions, who or whom is used for a person or a group of people; what is used only for things; and which is used for a person or an object.

Look at these sentences.  
- I poured **myself** a cup of coffee.  
- I, **myself** am to blame for the confusion.  
- Priya loves singing to **herself**.  
- She **herself** does not seem too worried about her results.  
In the first sentence of each pair, the highlighted pronoun (**myself, herself**) receives the action of the subject (**I, Priya**). In the second sentence of each pair, the highlighted pronoun is used for emphasis.  

A pronoun that indicates that the action of the subject reflects, or turns back, on the subject itself is called a **reflexive pronoun**.  

A pronoun that is used for the sake of emphasis is called an **emphasising pronoun**.  

Now, look at these sentences.  
- **Each** of the boxes was opened.  
- I haven't read **either** of the books.  
- **Neither** of them will admit to having broken the vase.  

**NOTE**:  
Distributive pronouns are always followed by the verb in the singular.  

In each of the sentences, the highlighted pronoun indicates people or things individually, rather than as a group.  

Pronouns that show that people or things are taken singly or in separate groups are called **distributive pronouns**. They are always singular and are, therefore, followed by the verb in the singular.  

Read these sentences.  
- **One** must preserve one's traditions.  
- **Many** had proposed the idea, but few voted in its favour later.  

In the above sentences, the highlighted pronouns refer to nouns that are understood or implied rather than stated in particular.  

**NOTE**:  
Remember, when using the pronoun one, you must be consistent. We cannot say—One tries to help his parents.  

Pronouns that do not refer to any person in particular are called **indefinite pronouns**. Some other indefinite pronouns are **several, all, both, anyone, everyone, someone, everybody** and **nobody**.  

Now, look at these sentences.  
- The two sisters always try to help **one another**.  
- They waved goodbye to **each other** as they parted.  

The highlighted pronouns indicate that two or more people carry out an action and receive its effect at the same time.  

Pronouns that denote mutual action are called **reciprocal pronouns**.

A pronoun that relates a noun that comes before it to a group of words that follow is called a **relative pronoun**.  

The most frequently used relative pronouns are **who, whose, whom, which** and **that**. Let us learn more about the uses of each.  

**NOTE**:  
A relative pronoun should always be placed close to its antecedent. We say—The *boy* **who** is in the train is my brother.  
We do not say—The **boy** is my brother **who** is in the train.  

The relative pronouns **who** and **whom** are used only for people, irrespective of gender or number. Look at these sentences.  
- Students **who** forget to bring their notebooks will not be allowed to sit for the test.  
- The teacher **whom** you were supposed to meet has left already.  

Note that **who** is used when the relative pronoun functions as the subject, whereas **whom** is used when the pronoun is the object.  

- We use **whose** to refer to both people and non-living things. For example,  
    - The bicycle **whose** lamp was missing belongs to Ratul.
    - The teacher scolded the students **whose** results were poor.  

- We can use **which** for non-living things and animals. For example,  
    - The sparrows **which** lost their home in the storm are building a new nest.
- The relative pronoun **that** usually refers to non-living things. For example,  
    - We were the first to find the clues **that** would lead us to the hidden treasure.  

**NOTE**:  
Sometimes the relative pronoun may be omitted. For example,  
- This is the house in which my father was born.  
- This is the house my father was born in.  
Did you notice that the position of the preposition has changed?

## Exercise B  

At dawn, we started to climb the mountain road. '**This** is an ancient road. It leads to a monastery and a temple. **These** were built about two hundred years ago,' said my guide Kabu, pointing to some low huts by the roadside.  

On either side of the narrow track grew many unfamiliar flowers. '**What** are these flowers called?' I asked.  

'**These** are orchids and **those** in the distance are rhododendrons,' said a local youth who had joined us.  

'**This** is proving to be an even better trek than I had expected,' I said to my friend.  

'Yes, indeed,' he replied. '**Who** is that fellow racing up at a great pace while we're panting behind him?'  

I looked at the man in front wondering at his incredible speed.  

Suddenly, there was a sudden shout.  

'Look at **that**,' said my guide pointing at a distant peak. '**That** lies Mount Everest. In our country, we also call it Sagarmatha. **Which** of you would like to try to climb that peak?'  

We looked at each other and shook our heads. **That** seemed like an impossible dream.
