# Transitive and Intransitive Verbs  

We know that there can be no sentences without verbs. A verb tells us—  
- what a person or thing does—She **designs** clothes.  
- what is done to a person—The girl **was scolded** by her mother.  
- what a person or thing is—The tiger **is** a magnificent animal.  

A verb can consist of more than one word. For example,  
- The boys **are playing** hockey.  
- The actors **were rehearsing** a play.  

Now, look at this sentence.  
- My mother **polished** the vase.  
In this sentence, the **vase** receives the action of the verb.  

The person or thing that receives the action of the verb is called the **object**.  

When a verb has an object that receives the action of the verb, it is called the **transitive verb**.  

Now, read this sentence.  
- The child **cried** aloud.  
In this sentence, there is no object. The action stops at the doer. No one receives the action of the verb.  

When a verb has no object, it is called the **intransitive verb**.  

Most transitive verbs take a single object. But some transitive verbs like **give, ask, promise, tell,** etc., take two objects—**a direct object** and **an indirect object**. For example,  
- My father *gave* **me** a cycle.  

The direct object of the verb is **cycle** and the indirect object is **me**.  

The person or thing that receives the action of the verb is called the **direct object**.  

The person or thing to whom or on whom the action of the verb is done or who receives the direct object is called the **indirect object**.  

**NOTE**:  
- The direct object of the verb can be identified by asking the question who/what after the verb. The indirect object is identified by asking the question to/for/from + whom/what after the verb. For example,  
    - The students gave the teacher (indirect) a bouquet of flowers (direct).
- Sometimes, the subject and the object refer to the same person or thing. We then say that the verb has been used reflexively. For example,  
    - The man hurt himself.
- Do remember that most verbs can be used both transitively as well as intransitively. For example,  
    - The lions fought the elephants.
    - The soldiers fought bravely.
- Some verbs like **come, go, fall, die, sleep** can never be used transitively.

**Verbs of Incomplete Predication**  

Read these sentences.  

- A dog **barks**.  
- People **laugh**.  
- Jackals **howl**.  

These sentences make complete sense. We do not need more words to complete the sense of the verbs.  

Now, read these groups of words.  

- The boy **appears**.  
- She **is**.  
- She **seems**.  
- The child **continued**.  

None of these groups of words make sense. We have to add more words to complete the sense of the verbs.  

Read these sentences now.  

- The boy *appears* **cheerful**.  
- She *is* **focussed**.  
- She *seems* **sad**.  
- The child *continued* **jumping**.  

Thus, we see that sometimes, intransitive verbs require more words to make complete sense, and to complete the predicate. Such verbs are called verbs of incomplete predication. The word or words required to make the sense complete is called the **complement of the verb**.  

In the above examples, the underlined complements tell us something about the subject. So these are called **subjective complements**.  

We know that transitive verbs have objects. However, sometimes just an object is not good enough. Read this sentence.  
- The members elected Gagan.  

We need something more to complete the predicate. For example,  
- The members elected Gagan **secretary**.  
Here, the word **secretary** is referring to Gagan, the object of the verb. So **secretary** is the **objective complement**.
