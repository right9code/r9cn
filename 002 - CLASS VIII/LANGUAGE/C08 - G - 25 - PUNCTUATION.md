# Punctuation  

The marks of punctuation are used in written text to make the meaning clear. You have learnt about the commonly used punctuation marks. They are—  
- full stop (.)  
- comma (,)  
- mark of interrogation (?)  
- mark of exclamation (!)  
- quotation marks or inverted commas ('...')  

Let us study about them in detail.  

**Full Stop**  
The **full stop**, or **period**, is the most important punctuation mark. It marks the end of an assertive or an imperative sentence. For example,  
- My name is Geetha. I am a teacher.  
- Go away.  

**Comma**  
The **comma** is used—  
- to separate three or more words or groups of words in a series. For example,  
    - Jaya, Sujatha, Vijay and Kamala are playing in the garden.
    - Rich and poor, young and old, men and women, walked by the monument.
- to set off a noun of address. For example,  
    - Father, I need your help.
    - Sit down, Vinay.
- before **please** and **thank** when they are placed at the end of a sentence. For example  
    - Could you pass me the salt, please?
    - Will you have tea? No, thank you.
- after expressions like **for example, however, moreover, that is, in fact**. For example,  
    - She is young. However, she is hardworking.
- to separate expressions like yes, no, oh and well. For example,  
    - Yes, we will arrive next week.
- to set off expressions that interrupt the flow of a sentence. For example,  
    - It is, as far as I can see, a good idea.
- to set off introductory phrases or subordinate clauses from the rest of the sentence. For example,  
    - After dinner, we went for a walk.
    - After she baked cookies, she baked a cake.
- to separate parts of an address. For example,  
    - 20, MG Road
- to separate parts of a date from the other words following the date. For example,  
    - On 12 January 1994, they met each other.
- to separate a statement from a question tag. For example,  
    - You did meet him, didn't you?
- to separate direct speech from the rest of the sentence. For example,  
    - Mamta said, 'I will go to the market.'
- after adverbs, if they are placed at the beginning of a sentence. For example,  
    - Fortunately, no one was injured.  

**Mark of Interrogation**  
- The **mark of interrogation** is used at the end of an interrogative sentence. For example,  
    - What do you need?
    - Can you wait for me?
- The **mark of interrogation** is also used at the end of a tag question. For example,  
    - We are meeting them on Sunday, aren't we?
    - You didn't finish that cake, did you?  

**Mark of Exclamation**  
- The **mark of exclamation** is used at the end of an exclamatory sentence. For example,  
    - What a pleasant surprise!
    - It was shocking!
- The **mark of exclamation** is also used after an interjection. For example,  
    - Hurrah!
    - Alas!  

**Capital Letters**  
A **capital letter** is used—  
- at the start of every sentence. For example,  
    - **S**he is a teacher.
- at the start of every sentence within quotation marks. For example, Jugal said, '**W**e will meet you tomorrow.'  
- with names of people and places. For example, the ***T*aj *M*ahal**, ***A*ustralia**, ***U*ncle *P*eter**, ***D*r *R*aj *K*umar**, ***N*ew *D*elhi**.  
- for the pronoun **I**. For example,  
    - *I* have to get up early.
- For the initials in people's names. For example, ***R* *K* Mehta**, ***P* *T* *U*sha**.  
- for the names of the days of the week and the months of the year. For example, ***Tuesday***, ***D*ecember**.  
- for the names of festivals and holidays. For example, ***I*ndependence *D*ay**, ***C*hristmas**.  
- for the names of books, films and plays. For example, ***A**lice in **W**onderland*, ***T**he **M**ask of **Z**orro*, ***H**amlet*.  
- for nationalities and languages. For example, ***C*hinese**, ***B*ritish**.

**Inverted Commas or Quotation Marks**  
**Inverted commas** or **quotation marks** are used to separate direct speech from the rest of the sentence. For example,  
- The lady said, 'I shall go home.'  
- What am I to you? he asked.  

**NOTE**:  
Inverted commas are also sometimes used when we are mentioning the name of a book or an article in a sentence. For example,  
- Have you read 'Oliver Twist'?

**Apostrophe**  
The **apostrophe** is used—  
- to show possession or belonging. The position of the apostrophe indicates singular or plural possession. For example,  
    - The girl's dress is pink. (referring to **one girl**)
    - The girls' dresses are pink. (referring to **many girls**)
- to show joint possession. For example,  
    - Jane and Jake's mother won the award.
- in contractions. For example,  
    - It's (It is) hot today.
    - Don't (Do not) litter the park.  

**NOTE**:  
Look at these words that are often confused.  
- we're (we are)  
    - were (past of 'be')
- it's (it is/it has)  
    - its (possessive pronoun/ possessive adjective)  

**Semicolon**  
The semicolon represents a pause of greater importance than a comma. Let us learn the use of the semicolon. It is used—  
- to separate words or phrases in a list, when they contain commas. For example,  
    - She bought vegetables such as potatoes, onions and cabbage; fruits such as bananas, grapes and oranges; and some household items.
- to separate lengthy coordinate clauses where the conjunction is omitted. For example,  
    - The snow fell; the winds blew; the sky grew dark; we huddled indoors.
- to separate a series of loosely related clauses. For example,  
    - Today we win what tomorrow we lose; today we search what tomorrow we abandon; today we desire what tomorrow we shun.  

**NOTE**:  
- There are two other punctuation marks that can be used to add extra information after a clause. These are the colon (:) and the dash (—). For example,  
    -  There are two parts of a sentence—the subject and the predicate.
    -  We collected a lot of things from the beach: shells, pebbles, birds' eggs and bottles.
- Hyphens (-) are used to join two or more words together. For example, door-to-door salesman, ninety-four balloons.

## Exercise D  

The children were to be driven, as a special treat, to the sands at Jagborough. Nicholas was not to be of the party; he was in disgrace. Only that morning he had refused to eat his wholesome bread-and-milk on the seemingly frivolous ground that there was a frog in it. Older and wiser and better people had told him that there could not possibly be a frog in his bread-and-milk, and that he was not to talk nonsense. He continued, nevertheless, to talk what seemed nonsense, and described with much detail the colouration and markings of the alleged frog. The dramatic part of the incident was that there really was a frog in Nicholas' basin of bread-and-milk; he had put it there himself, so he knew something about it. The sin of taking a frog from the garden and putting it into a bowl of wholesome bread-and-milk was talked about at great length, but the fact that stood out clearest in the whole affair, as it presented itself to the mind of Nicholas, was that the older, wiser and better people had been proved to be in error in matters about which they had been very confident.
