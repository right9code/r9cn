# The Sentence  

You have learnt that a sentence is a group of words that makes complete sense. A sentence always has at least one finite verb. A sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop, a question mark or an exclamation mark.  

**Subject and Predicate**  
A sentence consists of two parts—the **subject** and the **predicate**.  

In a sentence, the subject may be the person or thing that does the action or the one on whom the action is performed. For example,  
- **The swimmer** ***dived*** into the pool.  
- **The spacecraft** ***was*** launched.  

In the first sentence, the subject is the **swimmer** who does the action; and in the second sentence, the action is performed on the subject, that is, the **spacecraft**. The rest of the sentence, excluding the subject, is known as the predicate.  

**NOTE**:  
A finite verb is a verb that shows the person and number of the subject. For example,  
- Birds fly south for the winter.  
- Ravi's parrot flies in and out of its cage.  

**NOTE**:  
To identify the subject of the sentence, we usually ask the question who or what before the verb. In the given examples, we can identify the subject by asking—  
- Who dived into the pool?  
- What was launched?  

The **subject** of the sentence is the part which denotes the person or the thing we are talking about.  

The **predicate** is that part of the sentence which tells us about the subject. The predicate will always include the verb.  

The subject usually comes at the beginning of a sentence and is followed by the predicate. However, there are exceptions.  
- In lines of poetry, the subject might come after the predicate.  
    - Out of the sea came **he**!
- In questions, the subject comes after the helping verb.  
    - **Can** I be of any help?
- In imperative sentences, the subject is often implied or understood.  
    - **(You)** Go home as soon as you finish this work.  

The subject of a sentence may be one or several words. The word that is most important in such a phrase is then called the **subject-word**. Look at these sentences where the subjects have been highlighted and the subject-word underlined in each case.  

- ***The weather*** was hot.  
- **The hot *weather*** caused the death of many birds.  
- **The *weather*, being sultry**, spoilt our picnic plans.  

In the second and third sentences above, the subject-words have been qualified by an adjective (**hot**) and a group of words that acts as an adjective (**being sultry**), respectively. **The** is also considered to be an attribute.  

**NOTE**:  
The subject-word is always a noun, or a group of words that acts as a noun. For example,  
- To err is human. (infinitive acting as noun)  
- Walking is excellent exercise. (gerund acting as noun)  

The adjective or the adjective phrase that qualifies the subject-word in a sentence is called the **attribute**.

Like the subject, the predicate of a sentence is usually made up of more than one word.  

Sometimes, the verb in a sentence may be qualified by an adverb or a group of words that works as an adverb. Look at these sentences where the verb has been underlined and the qualifying word or group of words has been highlighted.  
- Ravi **runs** fast.  
- We **ran** all the way.  

An adverb or an adverb phrase that qualifies the verb is called the **adverbial qualification**.  

**NOTE**:  
If the predicate of a sentence is only one word, that word will be a verb.  
For example,  
- Lions roar.  
- A penguin brays.  

You have learnt that a verb in a sentence may be used transitively or intransitively. When the verb is used transitively, it may take one or more objects.  

**NOTE**:  
When a verb requires an object to receive its action, we say the verb is used transitively. A verb whose meaning is complete without an object is said to be used intransitively. For example,  
- The little dog is chasing a rat. (transitive)  
- The rat is running away as fast as possible. (intransitive)  

The person or thing that receives the action of the verb is called the **object** of the verb.  

Look at these sentences.  
- Sarita **is reading** a book.  
- I **love** peanut butter sandwiches.  
- Tara **met** her childhood friends after ages.  
In the above sentences, the verb has only one object—the direct object, which we identify by asking the question who/what after the verb.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- Manisha ***is making*** **tea** for ***her guests***.  
- Akshay ***gave*** **a beautiful pendant** to **his mother**.  
- The children ***received*** **a letter** from ***their father***.  
In the above sentences, the verbs take two objects—direct and indirect. The **indirect object** is identified by asking the question **to/for/from + whom/what** after the verb.  

Intransitive verbs do not have objects, but sometimes they require a word or a group of words to complete their sense. Look at these examples.  
- Mira ***is*** **a doctor**.  
- Romit ***seems*** **to be a studious boy**.  
The highlighted words in the above sentences are required to make the sentences complete and meaningful. They tell us something more about the subjects of the verbs.  

A word or a group of words that is required to complete the sense of a verb, and that tells us something about the subject of the verb is called the **subjective complement**.  

Now, read these sentences. Note that in the absence of the highlighted words, the sentences would sound incomplete.  
- I ***appointed*** him captain.  
- They ***proved*** me wrong.  
Here, the words **captain** and **wrong** tell us something about the object of the verbs, namely **him** and **me**.  

A word or a group of words that is required to complete the sense of the predicate and that tells us more about the object of the verb is called the **objective complement**.

**Kinds of Sentences**  
You have learnt that a sentence can be a statement, a question, a request, a command, a denial or a response.  

Read these sentences.  
- I like mangoes.  
- It is not raining.  
- Our pet dog is very playful.  
All these sentences state something.  

An **assertive sentence** makes a statement. It is also known as a **declarative sentence**.  

**NOTE**:  
An assertive sentence begins with a capital letter and ends with a full stop (.).  

Assertive sentences are of two kinds—**affirmative** and **negative** sentences. Look at these sentences.  
- The knife is blunt.  
- The knife is not sharp.  
The two sentences convey the same meaning, but one does so negatively, while the other states it positively.  

A sentence that tells us that something exists or something is happening is called an **affirmative sentence**. An affirmative sentence is also known as a **positive sentence**.  

A sentence that states that something does not exist or that something is not happening is called a **negative sentence**.  

**NOTE**:  
Negative sentences usually contain not or no.  

Read these sentences that ask questions.  
- Where is my umbrella?  
- Has the gardener sown the seeds?  

An **interrogative sentence** asks questions.  

There are two kinds of interrogative sentences—**yes** or **no** questions and **wh- questions**.  

Read these sentences.  
- Can you swim?  
- Does your brother play tennis?  

**NOTE**:  
An interrogative sentence always ends with a question mark (?).  

When we ask these questions, we expect the answer to be **yes** or **no**, so they are called **yes or no questions**.  

We use helping verbs like **be, have, do, can, should, may** or **will** to ask yes or no questions. In such questions, the helping verb comes before the subject and the main verb comes after the subject.  

For example,  
- **Can *you* read** out this story?  
- **Are *the Guptas* coming**?  

Now, read these sentences.  
- **Where** did you go yesterday?  
- **What** have you done to my phone?  

Questions that begin with **why, what, when, which, who, where, whom** and **how** are called **wh- questions**.  

Like yes or no questions, in wh- questions too, the helping verb comes before the subject and the main verb comes after the subject.  

**NOTE**:  
Sometimes, what, who or which may act as the subject of the sentence. For example,  
- Who committed this mistake?  
- Who left the lights on?  

Now, read these sentences.  
- Go away. **(order)**  
- Please close the window. **(request)**  
- Study well. **(advice**)  

A sentence that gives a command or an order, makes a request or gives advice is called an **imperative sentence**.  

In an imperative sentence, the subject is usually left out. The subject is understood to be **you** in most sentences. For example,  
- **(You)** Apologise right now.  
- **(You)** Go away.  

Now, read these sentences.  
- What a lovely painting!  
- What a miss!  

A sentence that expresses strong feeling is called an **exclamatory sentence**.  

**NOTE**:  
Not all imperative sentences are necessarily commands. They can be social expressions. For example,  
- Get well soon.  
- Have a nice day.  

**NOTE**:  
An exclamatory sentence always ends with an exclamation mark (!).

**Transformation of Sentences**  
You have learnt that it is possible to change the form of a sentence without entirely changing the meaning. Look at these pairs of sentences that convey the same meaning.  
- How sweet the song is! (**exclamatory**)

- Aren't elephants very intelligent? (**interrogative**)  
    - Elephants are very intelligent. (**assertive**)
- He did the work. (**affirmative**)  
    - He did not fail to do the work. (**negative**)
